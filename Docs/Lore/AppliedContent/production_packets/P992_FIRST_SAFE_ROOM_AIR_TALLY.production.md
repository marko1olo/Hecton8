### In-Game Codex

The first shelter is safe only in the narrowest sense: it has not failed yet.

Pressure can hold while air goes bad. A scrubber cartridge can show a finger sweep without proving capacity. A fan rail can be seated and still move too little air. A carbon strip can brown at the edge before the room feels dangerous. That is why the player reads the cabinet instead of trusting the word "safe."

Old worker-room marks make the system colder. Pressure bunks counted air cycles because breathing was already a managed resource before the Great Tide. Black Keel's oxygen welfare addendum keeps the same logic alive in 2190: air can be acknowledged, metered, billed, and still not treated as rescue.

### External Site Article

# First Safe Room Air Tally

## Safety Is Not Cleanliness

The first sealed room on HECTON-8 should not feel like a reward screen. It should feel like a machine that has made one promise and no more: the water is outside for now.

Pressure is the easiest part to mistake for safety. A hatch can seal. A wall can stop flexing. A status light can turn a tired green. None of that means the air is clean, circulating, dry, or good for clear decisions. The first shelter teaches a better rule: a safe room is a system under load, not a mood.

The air cabinet is where that rule becomes readable. The scrubber face has a finger-swept lane through dust, which proves someone checked the surface, not the remaining life. The carbon strip has browned at one edge, which points at carbon dioxide before panic arrives. The condenser drain carries salt crust. The fan rail has a clean break in its dust where it moved, but not enough proof that it moved well.

## Air Becomes Accounting

Black Keel does not need to sound cruel for the room to feel cruel. Its oxygen welfare addendum is almost polite: support billable after recovery. The phrase makes air part of the same machinery as cargo, lien relief, quarantine volume, and receiver custody. A breathing operator is still mass, still cost, still a line waiting for a window.

That matters because the colony used the same grammar before it drowned. Pressure bunks counted air cycles. Masks had clips. Scrubbers had cards. Routine survival was already written as inspection, consumption, reset, and signoff. The Great Tide did not invent that logic. It exposed what happened when the logic stayed calm after the people needed panic.

## What The Room Proves

The room proves that HECTON-8 is not built from abstract horror. It is built from small things that have to work in the right order. Seal first. Circulation next. Scrubber proof after that. Humidity, carbon dioxide, reserve oxygen, relay contact, and exit route after that. Skip one link and the room can keep its label while the operator loses judgment.

This gives the opening a different shape. The player is not reading lore because a database wants attention. The player is reading because a stale room can kill without making a dramatic sound. A brown strip, a salt ring, a missing mask clip, and a billing line are enough to turn atmosphere into evidence.

## How It Plays

The first shelter should let the player breathe and make them suspicious at the same time. It buys time, not innocence. It teaches that "holding" is not "safe," "checked" is not "proved," and "support" is not "rescue."

When the player leaves that room, they should understand the contract better. The ocean is not the only pressure. Air has a chain of custody. A body has a mass class. A minute of clear thinking has a maintenance history. HECTON-8 becomes frightening because survival is physical, priced, and inspectable.
