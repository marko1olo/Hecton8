### External Site Article

# HECTON-8 Pressure Ladder

## The Floor Is Rare

HECTON-8 is an ocean moon, not a staircase with scenery painted on each step. The useful ground appears in broken places: limestone shelves, collapsed industrial ledges, brine faults, vent scars, ridge backs, and service basins cut or occupied by old machinery. Between those places is water that changes the rules before it changes the view.

This is why a depth number matters less than what the depth does to the route. Pressure changes seals. Salt changes instruments. Brine changes buoyancy. Heat changes chemistry. Old Deep Reach construction changes what counts as a wall, a tool, a corpse, or an asset.

## Five Working Bands

The 0-250 m photic shelf is bright enough to read. That brightness is useful and dangerous. It lets the player see coral-like growth, limestone, route hardware and old evacuation damage before the descent takes color away. It can also make a cracked seal feel less urgent than it is.

The 250-1200 m industrial shelf is where human work becomes terrain. Cable reef, collapsed service corridors, module edges, pipe fields and salvage cuts turn infrastructure into a route. The player is no longer crossing a natural seabed. They are moving through a failed factory that the ocean has learned to use.

The 1200-2800 m brine canyon is the first place where "down" stops feeling honest. Density layers distort buoyancy and acoustic returns. A false floor can appear on instruments before it exists under the hull. Good navigation starts to mean reading the water, not only the map.

The 2800-4300 m abyssal machine field is pressure, silt and repair ecology. Atlas-altered materials begin to behave like maintenance surfaces. Biology carries signal, shell growth seals wrong fractures, and old machines keep asking the ocean to become infrastructure.

The 4300-5600 m Atlas basin is named carefully here because public text should not pretend to know every ending. It is the deepest pressure band, the place where pressure glass, Atlas infrastructure and old authority marks matter most. A route that reaches it is no longer only looking for salvage. It is choosing what kind of proof can leave the moon.

## The Guide Is A Warning, Not A Map

Depth bands do not grant safety. A shallow cave can be dark. A bright shelf can kill. A deep vent can be the only place to finish a pressure-rated repair. HECTON-8 rewards players who read evidence instead of color: salt bloom on a hatch, density shimmer under a lamp, a relay tone bending in brine, a pressure gauge that fails slowly instead of loudly.

The ladder is there so the player can learn the ocean's grammar. Every descent asks a different question: what keeps the route open, what makes the instrument lie, what material survives this pressure, and what proof is still worth carrying back?
