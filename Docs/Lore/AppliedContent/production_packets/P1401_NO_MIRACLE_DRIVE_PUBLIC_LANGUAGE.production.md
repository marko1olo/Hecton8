## Source Brief

This packet is a player-facing transit-language correction, not a technology spec. It exists because HECTON-8 must stay no-FTL, no-ansible, no-reactionless-rescue, while still feeling like a mature interstellar frontier.

Player use: turns "why no one comes" from a contrivance into a readable physical and legal fact. Light, packets, contracts, claims, and threats move cheaply. Human mass, pressure hardware, braking rights, quarantine clearance, and rescue lift do not.

Content tone: clear, hard, in-universe, readable. The text names the common false words because players and fictional workers would use them as slang, then rejects them as route facts.

Forbidden facts: no working FTL, no slipspace drive, no jump gate, no ansible, no instant Sol rescue, no reactionless thruster, no magical rescue carrier, no claim that Xenon-Omega powers superluminal travel, no final ending disclosure, no runtime/import/publication/native review proof.

### In-Game Codex

HECTON-8 has no faster-than-light rescue. The setting uses near-light infrastructure: beam arrays, pellet lanes, fusion-electric drive stages, shield mass, radiator margins, magsail braking, aerobrake windows, relay packets, and receiver custody. These systems can move expensive cargo across interstellar distance, but they need preparation, route ownership, heat budget, braking rights, and time.

That is why Black Keel can know an operator is alive and still fail to act like a loyal rescue ship. It has a local receiver role, a claim-pool ledger, a fuel and mass window, quarantine rules, and priority hooks. The same civilization that can authenticate a distress packet can leave the body below.

Old words survive as jokes, lies, marketing, or bad public memory. "Jump" means the contract skipped an explanation. "Slipspace" is fiction inside fiction. "Instant recall" is how a clerk describes a message they do not have to ride inside. A Marauder reads the metal: who pushed, who braked, who owned the receiver slot, and who never paid for a person to stop here.

### External Site Article

The first bad version of HECTON-8 is easy to write: a valuable material powers a miracle drive, the company owns the miracle, and the survivor waits because the company is cruel. That version is smaller, weaker, and wrong.

The stronger version is colder. Humanity reaches Aegir without cheating distance. Beam arrays spend power into sail schedules. Pellet lanes feed mass into fusion-electric stages. Shields die slowly in dust. Radiators make every kilogram an argument. Magsails and aerobrake skins spend years of speed back into a local system. Relays move claims, proof, orders, and threats because light is cheap compared with bodies. None of this creates a rescue button.

That physical rule gives HECTON-8 its shape. A message can leave the ocean and matter later. A contract can reach a ledger before a ship reaches the moon. Deep Reach can inherit a right, Keelmark can hold a lien, Tau Ceti can receive a proof packet, and Black Keel can keep a local receipt while the operator is still underwater. The horror is not absence of civilization. Civilization arrived as schedules, ownership, and accounting first.

This is why public language matters. Words like "jump," "warp," and "slipspace" make distance feel decorative. HECTON-8 needs distance to stay physical. The player should learn to distrust clean miracle language and read machinery instead: a worn braking plate, a closed mass-window, a relay tariff, a radiator margin, a custody meter. Those are the verbs of the setting. They explain why the ocean can be documented, profitable, and still lethal.

### Field Note

If someone sells you a miracle route, check the brake account. Speed is cheap to brag about. Stopping is where the bodies get priced.
