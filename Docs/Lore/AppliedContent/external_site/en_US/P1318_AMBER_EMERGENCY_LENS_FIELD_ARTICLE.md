# Amber Emergency Lens

Amber was chosen for pressure-base emergency lamps because it survives haze. Red vanishes quickly in dirty water and white turns every suspended particle into noise. Amber holds its edge through condensation, brine mist and old scratched viewport glass. That practical choice made the lens one of the best witnesses left in a drowned corridor.

The cover is not just colored plastic. It is a ribbed diffusion lens with a tough outer skin, a softer internal scatter layer and gasketed lips that should keep wet air out of the socket. When the seal fails slowly, minerals do not spread evenly. Salt gathers first in the rib troughs, then along the lower lip, then around clamp nicks. A single clean crack says impact. Milky crescents around the edge say pressure cycling. Brown-green grime below the gasket says the lamp spent time breathing with the room instead of staying sealed.

Players should treat the lens as route evidence. A clear amber line may still point toward a safe power path. A fogged line may point toward an older evacuation route that failed under condensation. A lamp wiped clean in the middle of an otherwise dirty hallway is suspicious: someone wanted the warning visible, or wanted the record altered. A lamp with scratches running across the ribs often means a gloved hand cleared it during alarm conditions. A lamp with only ridge-top abrasion is ordinary service wear.

For scavengers, the mistake is obvious and common. They break lenses for scrap, recover the lamp housings and lose the timeline. The useful question is not whether the lamp glowed. It is when the lamp stopped being trusted. On HECTON-8, a dead amber cover can still point toward the last open door, the last powered junction, and the person who walked away while the corridor was still blinking.
