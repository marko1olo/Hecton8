# Shallow Seagrass Root-Mat Substrate

The shallow root mat is one of the first living systems a new diver learns to read. It is bright enough to look welcoming and dense enough to lie. The upper layer catches Aegir light and releases a thin oxygen film during productive windows. Beneath that, the roots bind shell grit, limestone crumbs and broken coral analogues into a surface that behaves like a woven floor until weight, current or trapped gas changes the rule.

Deep Reach crews used root-mat edges as cheap route memory. Pinger lines snag there and stay visible. Old intake pipes become root anchors. A mat grown around a latch ring can keep a path readable after the sign is gone. That same habit makes the substrate dangerous. A route pinger braided into roots may show repeated safe passage, or it may show that the current kept dragging failed attempts into the same living knot.

Read color and tension together. Bright green blades over pale sand suggest active flow. Dark knots mean trapped silt, old metal or stagnant water. Shell grit aligned in one direction shows the current lane. A mat edge lifted like a lip may hide a hollow. The first lesson is simple: the shelf is readable because life is busy there, not because it is safe.
