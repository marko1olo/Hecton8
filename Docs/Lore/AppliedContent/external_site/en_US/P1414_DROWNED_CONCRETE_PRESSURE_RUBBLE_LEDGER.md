# Drowned Concrete Pressure Rubble Ledger

Drowned concrete is not scenery on HECTON-8. It is a dead building still speaking in material grammar. A pale chunk on the seabed might be ordinary stone, but pressure-rated colony concrete carries human geometry even after the room is gone: straight voids where gasket frames sat, orange shadows where reinforcement corroded away, ceramic aggregate that catches light differently from limestone, and shell sand packed into cracks by years of slow flooding.

The first useful question is not 'can I harvest it?' but 'what did it hold back?' A slab with lower green staining stood in pooled water before collapse. A clean shear face may be recent salvage, quake break or pressure failure. Rust without visible rods often means the reinforcing metal was stripped, dissolved or torn out by previous crews. Gasket dust around an edge tells you a hatch or pressure door used to bite there. These are route facts. They tell the player where a room was, where a pipe chase ran, what side was dry, and whether the next cut releases loot or ocean.

The material also carries labor history without a speech. Rushed concrete has different aggregate bands. Patch pours show colder color and worse bonding. Utility decks collect anti-slip grit at the edges. Living rooms collect warmer ceramic flakes and food-water stains. Deep Reach could rename an accident, but it could not make a bad pour age like a good one. When a Marauder scans rubble carefully, the ruined colony stops being a backdrop and becomes a readable body.

That is the intended fantasy: not picking up generic 'scrap concrete', but learning to see construction as evidence. The player can follow a rust shadow to a maintenance spine, avoid cutting the last compression piece, find a buried route board under shell sand, or decide that an intact wall fragment is more valuable as proof than as aggregate. The ocean erased the room. It did not erase the way the room failed.
