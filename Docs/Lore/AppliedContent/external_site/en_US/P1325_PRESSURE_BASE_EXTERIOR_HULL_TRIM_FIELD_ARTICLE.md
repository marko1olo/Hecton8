# Pressure Base Exterior Hull Trim

A pressure base exterior is designed to look calm from a distance. That is part of the lie and part of the engineering. Smooth off-white panels make a module readable in poor water. Dark ribs show where the shell expects force. Gasket channels mark the places where one room promised not to drown the next. Small inset lips mark the places a worker could still reach with a tool while wearing gloves, tired, and angry at the manual.

The useful information is in the order of those features. A rib that ignores a panel seam is structural. A trim strip that stops at a hatch is service language. Salt below a gasket channel means slow breathing. Salt below a painted plate means a larger migration path. Rust at a screw row says the fastener carried water. Clean paint inside a recessed lip says someone opened it late, after the surrounding skin had already aged.

For the player, exterior hull trim is a navigation instrument. It can point to safe cut lines, false-safe panels, hidden dry pockets, late repairs and rooms that Deep Reach marked sealed without proving they were empty. It can also reveal intent. A patched trim band with careful bolt spacing says maintenance expected to return. A welded strip across a service lip says someone decided access was no longer allowed. A perfect replacement band on an otherwise exhausted module says the official story touched the wall.

The mistake is treating trim as art noise. On HECTON-8 the edge is often more honest than the middle. A clean flat panel may only tell you what the company wanted seen. The bands around it tell you what the base actually needed to survive.
