# Nearlight Drive Stack Cutaway

A near-light ship in HECTON-8 is not a clean arrow with one impossible engine. The visible hull is the last serviced part of a route laid down years before the crew wakes. Barnard Yards training plates draw the stack in the order a worker actually pays for it: beam-sail push, pellet catch, fusion-electric throat, shadow shielding, radiator ribs, magsail spool, aerobrake skin and receiver custody.

Each stage has a different owner and a different way to fail. A beam array can sell departure before anyone has paid for arrival. A pellet lane can feed the burn and still bankrupt a cargo window if catch tolerances drift. The shield can survive a voyage by becoming thinner. Radiators can keep everyone alive by making every extra kilogram a question. The magsail and aerobrake accounts decide whether the ship is allowed to stop without burning the profit in its tanks.

This is how the human frontier stays large and cruel without breaking physics. Light carries claims, distress packets and legal threats far more cheaply than bodies. Deep Reach can inherit a right before it moves a hull. Black Keel can sit in the Aegir system and still behave like a contract terminal because it belongs to the local receiver stack, not to the trapped worker below it.

The cutaway is useful because it gives the player a way to read rescue as machinery. A pitted radiator rib, a closed braking ledger or a custody meter is not background detail. It is part of the answer to a simple question: who paid for this mass to stop here, and who did not?
