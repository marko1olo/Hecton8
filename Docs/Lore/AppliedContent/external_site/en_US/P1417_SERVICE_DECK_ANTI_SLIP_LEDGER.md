# Wet Service Deck Anti-Slip Ledger

The best floors in HECTON-8 are not clean. Clean floors would be lies. A drowned service deck should show the difference between design and use: the intended grid of raised tread, the practical stripe of yellow paint, the human arc where a boot kept turning, and the ocean's slow edits in salt and rust.

Anti-slip plate tells time through contact. The top of a ridge becomes pale where boots and dragged crates polished it. The trough between ridges grows darker when oil, coolant or brine settles there. Safety paint disappears first on the routes that mattered. Rust grows only where hands, boots, carts and water no longer disturb it. Those marks can point toward an airlock, a dry pocket, a dead end or a place where the official evacuation record no longer fits.

In the field, those marks become choices: trust the worn line, avoid the glossy trough, preserve a late scuff as proof, or follow yellow paint into a maintenance space that Deep Reach never put on tourist maps.

The floor is not dramatic. That is why it works. Under pressure, the most useful witnesses are usually the ones everyone stepped on.
