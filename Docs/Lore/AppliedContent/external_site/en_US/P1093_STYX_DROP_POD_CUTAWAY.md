# Styx Drop Pod Cutaway

> **Source:** *Black Keel* Descent Operations Manual, Annex D-44, "Contractor Bathy-Drop Delivery."
> **Annotation:** loss-adjustment note appended by Marek Ibarra, Keelmark Mutual.
> **Release:** public-safe cutaway. Claim-window keys, ascent charge signatures and insurer thresholds are held on separate sheets.

---

## 1. Classification

A Styx is a disposable bathy-drop shell rated to move one suited operator from the *Black Keel* to a legal worksite with the least mass an insurer will accept. Annex D-44 never uses the words shuttle, lifeboat or rescue capsule. There is no pilot station and no window. The operator lies locked in a Class-IV suit cradle while the shell handles the part of the descent that would kill an unsupported body.

The pattern is inherited. The original bus punched autonomous mining heads through Aegir storm bands. After 2147, when claim recovery became cheaper than rescue, the bus was re-certified for living cargo. The certification does not say safe. It says survivable under contracted parameters.

Ibarra's appended note supplies the figure: 88.4 percent survivable delivery, with weather, shell age, suit seal and operator biometrics all inside green margin. The same note files a failed drop as a delivery loss rather than a death. That reclassification is the annex's real content, and it is complete before the shell leaves the cradle.

## 2. Descent profile

Launch is from a rotary cradle inside the carrier. Final checks run faster than a person reads them: suit lock, spinal foam, helmet feed, scrubber reserve, ballast valve, uplink mast, acoustic pinger, claim hash, impact clock. Then the cradle throws.

The shell leaves blunt end forward on a magnetically assisted deorbit path, ablator heating before the operator feels drag. Guidance is deliberately crude. It accepts a corridor, burns through turbulence, and corrects only when the impact ellipse drifts outside the insured shelf. Every other discomfort is logged as an operator condition.

Water impact is the second launch. The sacrificial shock skirt breaks, the ablator cracks, the foam cradle crushes around the suit, and the ballast floods before the shell can bounce. A clean hit leaves the operator concussed and sinking. A bad hit puts the cylinder sideways and shears the mast at the hinge.

## 3. Systems that matter after impact

**Crush frame.** Ribbed titanium-carbide cylinder rated to hold a human-sized air pocket long after the shell stops being a vehicle. Intact, the crash site is shelter, tool locker and terminal.

**Ablator.** A reused shell carries patch resin, mismatched tile and scorch history. Uneven loss indicates storm shear outside the planned corridor. Resin around the mast indicates an earlier failed recovery.

**Acoustic stack.** Radio dies early in this water. The shell reports in low-frequency pulses and compressed packet bursts during window alignment. A cracked mast returns messages late, partial, or suspiciously clean.

**Survival bus.** Lamp, cradle heat, hand-tool trickle charge, one scrubber purge, one small terminal. It cannot power a base or tow a diver.

**Recovery interface.** A keyed sleeve for winch hardware, buoy links, charge adapters and carrier-side authentication. Contractor copies call it an ascent package. It is permission, geometry and a metal throat that may accept the right hook.

## 4. Failure vocabulary

**Gimbal lock.** Bad impact vector. The operator may live; the mast usually does not.

**Relay mast shear.** Acoustic noise still arrives. No clean uplink leaves.

**Compressor flood.** Equalisation swallowed ocean before the seals stabilised. Shelter alive, ascent chain dead.

**Heat-tile reserve local only.** The last trustworthy ablator margin was spent reaching the water.

**Recovery ring no-lock.** The *Black Keel* can register that something arrived and cannot certify what it may lift.

## 5. Why the pattern persists

A crewed rescue shuttle needs fuel, pilots, redundant pressure systems, hazard pay and a legal order to risk living crew inside a dead claim. A deep-rated drone costs more than the contractor it would retrieve, and this water ruins its circuitry inside a season. A full ascent vehicle needs launch mass, stable weather and orbital geometry Deep Reach keeps in-house.

A Styx needs one disposable shell, one signature and one carrier that can record delivery to the worksite. Ibarra's note closes on the recovery ring: a licensee can be alive and outside the recoverable mass class simultaneously, and Annex D-44 provides no form for that condition.
