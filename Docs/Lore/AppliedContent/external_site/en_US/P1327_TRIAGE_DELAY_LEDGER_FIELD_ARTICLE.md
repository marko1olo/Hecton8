# Triage Delay Ledgers

The medical bay on HECTON-8 did not fail only when the lights went out. It failed earlier, in tidy rows.

A triage delay ledger looks merciful at first glance. It has injury classes, oxygen-debt marks, stabilization notes, transfer windows and witness fields. It has the kind of compact writing people trust because it feels practical. Under pressure that practicality mattered. A medic could not write an essay while a hatch groaned and a pump spine hammered through the deck. They needed marks, codes, fast decisions.

The ugly part is what the codes allowed management to do. A worker with crushed ribs could become amber hold. A person who needed evacuation could become stabilized pending route clearance. A missing stretcher battery could become equipment state unresolved. A locked corridor could become no movement authority instead of nobody came for them. Every phrase kept the system clean while the room got worse.

That is why the physical bay matters. The ledger alone can lie by omission. The cot straps, oxygen rack, floor scuffs, hatch stamp, soaked pressure tape and witness hash make it answer questions. Was the patient truly stabilized, or did the same oxygen cylinder get reassigned three times? Did the route close before the injury, or was the route closed because the injury made the numbers inconvenient? Did a later hand rewrite the row above a waterline? Did a Marauder mark restore a name the ledger tried to turn into a category?

For the player, triage delay ledgers are a way to read harm without turning the colony into melodrama. HECTON-8 is strongest when the evidence is procedural and intimate at once. The ledger says the system had language for abandonment. The room says who paid for that language.
