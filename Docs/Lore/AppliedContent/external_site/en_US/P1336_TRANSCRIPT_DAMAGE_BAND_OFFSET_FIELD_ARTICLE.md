# Transcript Damage Band Offset

HECTON-8 treats voice logs as objects first and speech second. A transcript damage band is one of the tools that keeps that rule honest. It marks the part of a caption or transcript where the original audio is missing, masked or too damaged to quote. When it lines up with the cassette, hydrophone strip and notary mark, it is a warning. When it drifts away from those objects, it becomes evidence of handling.

The offset matters because a transcript can be repaired faster than a physical carrier. A recovery desk can smooth noise, move captions, hide a name and publish a version that looks official. The recorder hardware is less cooperative. Scratches, feed skips, salt bloom and clock ladder notches keep their order. If the black band arrives late, the reader can see the point where document custody separated from machine custody.

This article sits in the recorder-bay evidence chain. Audio fragment tags tell where a voice file claims to belong. Hydrophone strips tell what the room heard through metal and water. Cassette teeth and ink wheels tell how the carrier moved. The transcript damage band offset asks whether the readable words still belong to that carrier. It is not a grand secret; it is a timing error with a billable owner.

Publicly, Deep Reach can call the band a degraded caption segment. On Aegir, a Marauder with the strip in hand can call it something harder: the transcript was cleaned after the damage, and the cleaned version is trying to look older than it is.
