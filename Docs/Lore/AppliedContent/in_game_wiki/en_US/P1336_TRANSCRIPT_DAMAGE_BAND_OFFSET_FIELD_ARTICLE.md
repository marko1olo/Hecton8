# Transcript Damage Band Offset

A transcript damage band is supposed to admit what the audio lost. The useful one sits where the recorder, cassette edge or packet notary left a matching mark. A bad one floats. It covers a clean syllable, misses the scrape on the strip, or lands between two clock ladder ticks where no damage object can carry it. That offset is small on the screen and ugly in custody.

In a pressure-room recorder chain, the band has to line up with more than sound. The hydrophone strip may show a white scrape where the head dragged. The cassette teeth may skip a notch beside the same pressure spike. The ink wheel may break dry before the dropout or bloom wet after it. A packet notary can mask a name or coordinate, but it cannot move the scratch on the carrier. When the black band sits 1.8 seconds late, somebody made a cleaner transcript from a dirtier object.

Deep Reach filing language can hide the problem under "audio degradation." That phrase is cheap. A real offset has a measurable order: room event, strip damage, copy pass, notary mask, published transcript. If the published band follows the mask but not the strip, the source was handled after the room record already existed. If the band hides a route permission stamp while the machine marks stay readable, the omission is procedural, not accidental.

Field use is simple: compare the band before trusting the words. If the damage band matches the cassette scrape, keep the transcript in the evidence chain. If it trails the scrape, mark the transcript as a later copy. If it hides the one line that would connect a route hold to a person, look for the physical carrier before accepting the clean text.
