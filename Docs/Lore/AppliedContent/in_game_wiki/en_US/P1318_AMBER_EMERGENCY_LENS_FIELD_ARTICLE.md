# Amber Emergency Lens

Amber warning lenses were fitted over corridor strobes, airlock beacons, pump alarms and suit-locker fault lamps. Their ribbed glass-plastic covers diffused light through water haze and pressure glass, but the material also kept a record. Salt fog in the ribs shows repeated breathing of wet air. White powder at the lower lip shows standing seepage. Scratches that cross the rib direction usually come from a gloved wipe during a hurry, while scratches that follow the ribs are normal maintenance abrasion. In a dead base, the lens can tell whether a warning line was still powered, merely reflective, or deliberately cleaned after the event.

## Scanner

AMBER EMERGENCY LENS // Ribbed translucent cover from a pressure-base warning lamp. Salt fog inside the grooves means water reached the lamp before the circuit died. Do not read the glow alone; read the lens.

## Terminal

Lighting service note: amber strobes are route logic, not decoration. A clear lens marks live evacuation order. Milky ribs mark long condensation. Scratches across the ribs usually mean someone wiped the lamp after the alarm, not before it.

## Audio

The lamp kept blinking after the hall went quiet. Not bright enough to save anyone. Bright enough to show who walked past.

## Field Note

If the amber is too clean, ask who cleaned it. If the ribs are cloudy, ask how many wet breaths the room took before power died.
