# Salvage-Cut Cross-Section Field Article

A salvage-cut cross-section is an opened wound in a pressure structure. The useful part is not the hole. The useful part is the order: paint, primer, dark load-bearing metal, ceramic insulation, rubber seal, cable bundle, heat-browned edge, salt after the water found it. A player who reads that order can tell whether a panel failed, was repaired, was cut for access, or was stripped after everyone had stopped calling the module recoverable.

HECTON-8 hulls were not clean spaceship skins. A base wall could carry off-white paint, graphite ribs, sprayed ceramic backing, gasket lips, cable trays and patch plates from three maintenance periods. When a cutter opens it, the layers appear in cross-section. Fresh cut metal is darker and sharper. Old pressure cracks feather the primer before the cut reaches them. Ceramic insulation drinks water at the edge and turns chalky. Rubber gaskets smear instead of chipping. Cable bundles show copper, fiber and black jacket in one exposed bite.

For salvage, the edge is evidence. A burned line that runs over old salt means the cut came after flooding. Salt inside the insulation but not on the outer paint means water traveled through the wall before anyone opened it. A cable bundle sliced cleanly with no tagged isolation mark means the worker was moving fast or did not care what the cable still fed. If a gasket slice is glossy and compressed, the door or module was still being pressured when the cut was made. If the ceramic layer flakes into the room, the wall dried under air for a time before the next flood.

The scanner should not treat every cut as a loot prompt. Some cross-sections mark safe access into a service chase. Some warn that the visible path crosses live power, oxygen, coolant or data lines. Some are legal evidence: who cut first, who returned later, and which company tried to turn a rescue breach into a claimable salvage boundary. Marauders learn to read the burn direction before cutting deeper. The ocean does not erase a cutter line quickly. It salts it, stains it and lets small organisms decide which layer was easiest to inhabit.

The public story usually says opened wrecks are chaotic. They are not. A trained cutter leaves a grammar. Start notch. Slow corner. Pause where the tool met reinforcement. Wider burn where the operator lost cooling. A second pass that avoids a cable tray. A careless pass through the cable tray anyway. In a drowned colony, that grammar can matter more than the terminal beside it, because terminals lie in complete sentences and hull edges lie one layer at a time.

Black Keel payout clerks want mass, alloy class and custody. Deep Reach reports want failure category. The player needs something more immediate: can this edge be touched, cut, followed, sealed or ignored? A good salvage-cut cross-section answers with material. Paint says surface. Metal says load. Ceramic says heat and pressure. Rubber says seal. Cable says consequence. Burn says who came after the original accident.

## Field Note

Cut edges keep time better than clocks. Salt first, burn second means somebody came back after the room drowned.
