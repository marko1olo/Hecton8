# Brine Density Route Ledger

The Brine Stairs are not a cave staircase. They are layers of water heavy enough to behave like false ground: clear on top, thick below, sharp at the boundary. A sonar ping sent straight down can come back like a floor plate. A diver who believes it may drop into a heavier layer, lose trim, scrape a cable or sink under a white crust that was never solid.

The old colony learned to read the stairs by abusing small instruments. A side-ping shows whether a return is a wall, a ledge or a density curtain. A ballast trim tells whether the suit is floating in ordinary seawater or being held by brine. Temperature falloff marks where vent-warmed water slides across cold salt. Cable scars on the rock matter more than clean map lines, because cable does not flatter itself when it hits a layer.

For a Marauder, the ledger turns fear into procedure. A brine curtain can hide a route from predators and drones. It can cool overheated cutters. It can preserve an evac tag or a corpse record for years. It can also eat seals, bend sound and make a safe floor out of a lie. Read the ledger when entering the 2800-3600 m Brine Stairs: side-ping, trim, wait for drift, cross at the scar, and never spend the last ballast correction on a pretty reflection.

## Scanner

BRINE LEDGER // Density boundary ahead. Sonar return bends below the visible shelf; ballast response lag is expected. Side-ping the curtain before descent. Treat the shining floor as a moving fluid until two trims agree.

## Terminal

DEEP REACH ROUTE BOARD B3-17 // Do not land on the white return. It is a salt-crust skin over heavier brine. Mark trim correction, temperature falloff and cable scrape depth before crossing. Missing evac pods last pinged under the third curtain, not on the charted floor.

## Audio

If the floor looks polished, distrust it. Ping from the side, bleed ballast by touch, and do not let the brine write your depth for you.

## Field Note

Salt writes better maps than management. Follow the scratches, not the clean line.
