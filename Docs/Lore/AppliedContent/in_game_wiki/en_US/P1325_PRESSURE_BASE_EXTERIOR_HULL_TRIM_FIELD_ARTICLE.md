# Pressure Base Exterior Hull Trim

The exterior of a HECTON-8 pressure base is not a decorative shell. Its trim bands are the readable grammar of a module built to be serviced underwater. Off-white ceramic coating marks pressure skin. Dark graphite ribs show where load travels. Narrow gasket channels show where two volumes were meant to meet without sharing the ocean. Inset panel lips usually mean human access, not ornament.

Read the trim before cutting. A long rib that runs unbroken across a module tells you the wall is carrying pressure as one body. A broken rib beside clean salt streaks means the leak stayed local and may have left a pocket behind. A gasket channel packed with old mineral dust means the joint breathed slowly. A panel lip with fresh scratches means someone opened it after the room was already wet or after the official work order ended.

Good trim sheets make bases feel built instead of painted. They also make salvage less blind. A player can follow rib direction to avoid bad cuts, find service hatches by repeated gasket geometry, and read which modules were patched for return versus abandoned as evidence. The ocean hides labels first. It leaves edges.

## Scanner

EXTERIOR HULL TRIM // Off-white pressure skin, graphite rib band, recessed gasket channel. Salt streaks run below the service lip, not below the main plate. This is a maintained module edge, not random wreck plating.

## Terminal

HULL SKIN READ // Do not cut through trim bands until load path is mapped. Long dark ribs carry pressure, pale panels close volume, gasket channels mark where modules joined, and service lips often hide the last hand-access route.
