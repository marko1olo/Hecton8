# NEARLIGHT TRANSIT STACKS

> **Source:** Deep Reach Extraterrestrial Development Combine, Public Engineering Archive 2190 revision.  
> **Speaker:** Automated Propulsion Records, Black Keel custody mirror.  
> **Reviewer Note:** The public propulsion archive, 2190 revision. No vessel in it outruns light, and the route sheets show what is spent instead.

---

## 1. What Nearlight Means

No entry in this archive describes a vessel that shortens a route. Every entry describes what a route costs and who signed for it.

A nearlight vessel accelerates for a long time, rides a surveyed corridor, protects a fragile payload against radiation and time, then asks a receiver system to spend the energy required to stop it. Ran to Aegir measures roughly ten and a half light-years, and the route sheet for that lane runs to nine pages, of which one page is propulsion and eight are permissions.

The old public brochures call this “interstellar access.” The ship crews call it route debt. Every arrival is a chain of bills paid in advance: launch window, beam time, pellet stream, shadow-shield service, navigation witness, receiver slot, quarantine lane, cargo custody and insurance signature.

If one link fails, the message may still arrive. The body may not.

## 2. The Acceleration Stack

Deep Reach haulers use a layered drive stack because no single engine carries a colony economy across interstellar distance. The first push comes from infrastructure: launch beams, orbital tugs and staged mass drivers that throw the ship onto a narrow departure corridor. The ship then spends months or years trimming velocity with fusion-electric engines, magnetic sails and pellet-stream catches.

The visible drive is only the last part of the machine. The real stack includes heat exchangers the size of apartment blocks, wake sensors that read dust before it becomes a bullet, sacrificial foil shields, frozen crew vaults, cargo custody locks, and a guidance computer that treats a loose bolt like a court case. A bad kilogram at nearlight speed is not clutter. It is a weapon nobody meant to fire.

Xenon-Omega does not erase the years between stars. In current canon it is a pressure-process material used where ordinary substrates fail: high-field coils, radiation-hard sensor beds, receiver shutters, thermal switching plates and custody-sealed experimental components. Deep Reach sold it as route salvation because salvation language raises credit. Engineers wrote it down as a material with narrow tolerances and expensive consequences.

## 3. Stopping Is The Expensive Part

Acceleration is dangerous. Stopping is political.

A receiver must catch the ship without melting its own infrastructure, poisoning its quarantine chain or accepting cargo whose owner cannot be proven. That is why every major route keeps braking slots. A slot is not a welcome mat. It is a scheduled permission to consume receiver heat budget, magsail geometry, pellet-catcher time, tug capacity, quarantine volume and legal custody.

This is the part workers on HECTON-8 learned too late. A distress packet could be true, authenticated and loud. Black Keel could hear it. Aegir receivers could list it. Deep Reach offices could stamp it. None of that opened a slot large enough for a colony. The receiver did not ask who was scared. It asked what mass could be accepted, who owned it, what quarantine class it carried, and who would pay if it broke loose.

## 4. Bodies In Transit

Nearlight travel does not kill by spectacle. It wears people down by procedure.

Crews cross the long years in cold sleep, shield shifts, awake maintenance watches and scheduled medical interruptions. They wake with missing months in their memory, bruised joints, stale lungs, radiation-dose flags and the knowledge that everyone they left behind has been living at a different speed. A corporate form calls this “chronological displacement.” A deck medic calls it “coming back to a room that kept moving.”

Marauders know the same math in miniature. A Black Keel tender can bring a person into its books before it can bring that person out of the water. Debt moves at light. Hardware moves below it. Skin moves last.

## 5. Deep Reach Haulers

Deep Reach cargo ships are not silver explorers. They are long industrial arguments: shield cones, tank farms, receiver masts, cold vaults, radiator farms, cargo spheres, debt locks and a drive throat buried behind service decks nobody visits without a reason.

They carry refined material, sealed evidence, cryo labor, replacement modules, claim paperwork and enough automation to make a legal decision without a kind one. In port they look dead. Under thrust they look worse: a chain of hot machinery trying to keep a human-made promise smaller than physics.

The core worlds remember these ships as supply. HECTON-8 remembers them as the shape of departure. One arrived, loaded, signed and left. The ocean kept the rest.
