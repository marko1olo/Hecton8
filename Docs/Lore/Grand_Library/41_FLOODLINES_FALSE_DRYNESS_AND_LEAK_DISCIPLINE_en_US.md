# FLOODLINES, FALSE DRYNESS AND LEAK DISCIPLINE

> **Source:** Deep Reach habitat maintenance standard, Black Keel ingress-damage pricing table, Marauder floodline wall notes.  
> **Speaker:** Pressure-habitat technician, later annotated by crews who had to enter rooms the standard never expected to survive.  
> **Reviewer Note:** A dry room on HECTON-8 is not a promise. It is a current negotiation between hull, pressure, brine, power and whoever last lied about maintenance.

---

## 1. Dry Is A Temporary Arrangement

On HECTON-8, water is not outside. Water is waiting.

Deep Reach sold the first habitats as sealed architecture: ceramic pressure skins, gasketed service corridors, pump-backed airlocks, inspection gutters, emergency membranes and redundant drain paths. In the brochures, a room was either dry or flooded. In the colony, that distinction died before the first audit cycle.

A module could be dry at floor level and drowning in its ceiling trays. A corridor could hold air because the doors at both ends were warped into a lucky seal. A storage cabinet could be dry until a Marauder warmed it with a work lamp and woke the brine trapped in the insulation. A nursery could smell clean while a wall behind it sweated through one bolt hole every six minutes. The ocean did not need a breach large enough for drama. It only needed patience, salt and a pressure difference.

Old crews learned to treat dryness as a moving condition. They did not ask, "Is this room safe?" They asked, "What is keeping the water out right now, and what will change if I touch it?"

That question is still the beginning of leak discipline.

## 2. Floodlines Are Memory

A floodline is the mark water leaves when it has already won once.

Some lines are obvious: salt crust around the walls, rust tongues below conduit brackets, pale mineral lace on locker doors, a brown-black band where microbial film found an air-water boundary and made a living edge out of it. Others are quieter. A label curls only at the bottom. A floor screw blooms green while the rest stay dull gray. Dust sits too cleanly above a shelf because everything below it was rinsed away. A child-height scuff line stops at a door because the corridor on the other side filled first.

Deep Reach maintenance logs called these "historic wetting indicators." Marauders call them floodlines because a plain word keeps the insult in it. The room drowned. Somebody drained it. Somebody decided it was cheaper to reopen than rebuild. The wall remembers even when the lease file does not.

Floodlines matter because they show behavior, not just damage. A high line with clean lower walls means fast flooding and fast pumping. A low line with thick mineral teeth means slow seepage and long neglect. A line that rises behind equipment but drops near the door means bad slope, blocked drains or a crew that sealed the exit before sealing the leak. A line through an archive cabinet tells the player which records were underwater and which records survived by accident.

Good Marauders read floodlines before they read room names.

## 3. False Dryness

The most dangerous wet room is the one pretending to be dry.

False dryness happens when a room has lost visible water but kept the conditions that brought it in. Pumps can clear a floor while leaving pressure in a ceiling blister. Heaters can dry a panel face while insulation behind it stays sour and heavy. Emergency foam can trap brine against cable bundles until the copper turns to soft green meal. A cracked viewport can hold because a mineral skin formed over the wound, and then fail when a tool case bumps the frame.

There are also legal forms of false dryness. Black Keel may mark a compartment "dry-accessible" because a drone crossed it once. A buyer may price a recovered tool as clean because its exterior was wiped before custody. A Deep Reach archive may show a successful dewatering cycle and omit that the pump ran on borrowed power from a dead clinic. The words are not always lies. Sometimes they are just too small for the room.

Marauders distrust clean floors. They distrust quiet drains. They distrust any air pocket that has no reason to be alive. A room that admits it is wet is often kinder than a room that asks the player to believe in it.

False dryness is why crews carry dye tabs, chalk, humidity thread, cheap pressure stickers and bad jokes. The tools are small because the truth is usually small at first.

## 4. Leak Discipline

Leak discipline is not heroic repair. It is the order of operations that keeps a useful room from becoming a grave.

First: stop making the room worse. Do not cut a panel because it is in the way. Do not open a cabinet because it looks dry. Do not cycle a door until somebody knows which side has pressure, which side has water and which side has the better lie.

Second: find the source, the path and the future. Water entering from a cracked overhead pipe is a different problem from water climbing through a floor seam. Brine dripping from a cable tray may have traveled twenty meters before choosing that place to fall. A leak under pressure can seal itself with salt and then break when heat, vibration or a careless boot changes the terms. A leak is not a point. It is a route.

Third: mark the truth where the next person can see it. Marauder marks are ugly on purpose: arrows, time, depth, taste if someone risked a sample, pump status, "ceiling heavy," "do not warm," "drain talks," "seal belongs to door not wall." Pretty labels invite trust. Field marks invite suspicion, which is more useful.

Fourth: repair only what the mission can defend. A patch that holds long enough to retrieve medicine may be good. A patch that invites a crew to sleep under a loaded ceiling is murder with better handwriting. Deep Reach loved permanent language. Marauders prefer expiration times.

The best leak repair says what it is not strong enough to do.

## 5. Water As Evidence

Water is a witness that ruins most paperwork.

It moves dust, floats bodies, stains priority, eats signatures, preserves footprints in silt and deletes them with the next pressure cough. It can prove a door was open before a breach. It can prove a crate was moved after a room flooded. It can show that a claimed "sealed route" was used by someone with wet boots and no right to be there.

Black Keel values this when it helps custody. It ignores it when the stain lowers margin. Buyers prefer clean salvage because clean objects tell fewer stories. Courts prefer clean chains because water turns sequence into argument. The ocean prefers none of these things. It just keeps entering.

For the player, water evidence can become leverage. A mineral line on a safe can show when it was opened. A dried runnel across a floor can point to a hidden service void. A wet handle can betray another crew. A dry patch under a corpse can say the body was moved. A floodline through a memorial wall can make an old official version impossible.

This is why some crews erase water marks and why better crews copy them first.

## 6. What The Player Learns

The player learns that water is not background. It is pressure with handwriting.

A leak can open a route, close a route, expose a cache, spoil a battery, attract fauna, hide sound, falsify a map or prove that someone arrived before the player. A dry room can be refuge, trap, auctionable asset, legal risk or a temporary kindness from machinery that is almost out of patience.

Good play is not just sealing leaks. Sometimes the player drains a room to breathe. Sometimes they leave it wet because flooding is the only thing holding a bad door shut. Sometimes they cut a relief path before taking a prize. Sometimes they mark a truth they cannot afford to fix.

On HECTON-8, the ocean is not defeated by walls. It is delayed by competent habits. Every floodline is a sentence from an earlier failure, and every dry floor asks the same question: who is paying for this silence, and how long can they keep paying?
