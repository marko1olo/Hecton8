# FLOODLINES, FALSE DRYNESS AND LEAK DISCIPLINE

> **Source:** Deep Reach habitat maintenance standard, Black Keel ingress-damage pricing table, Marauder floodline wall notes, and pump-room return cards recovered from shallow annexes.  
> **Scope:** How dry rooms fail, how old water marks expose route history, and how leak handling changes salvage value, safety and evidence.  
> **Field use:** Read before trusting a dry floor, warming a cabinet, cycling a door, cutting a panel, or wiping a line of salt from a wall.

---

## 1. Dry Is A Current Condition

On HECTON-8, dry does not mean safe. Dry means water is being held somewhere else for now.

Deep Reach sold the first habitats as sealed architecture: ceramic pressure skins, gasketed corridors, pump-backed airlocks, inspection gutters, emergency membranes and redundant drain paths. In brochures, a room was either dry or flooded. On the colony floor, that split died before the first audit cycle.

A module can be dry at boot level and drowning in its ceiling trays, and a corridor holds air only because two warped doors jammed into a seal. Open a cabinet that reads clean and a work lamp warms the brine in its insulation within the hour. Berth row 44-B-6 smelled scrubbed for an audit cycle while one bolt hole sweated every six minutes. The sea does not need a breach. It needs pressure, salt and patience.

Old crews stopped asking whether a room was safe. They asked what was keeping the water out, what it was borrowing power from, and what would change when the next person touched it.

That is leak discipline before the repair kit comes out.

## 2. Floodlines

A floodline is the mark left by water that already won once.

Some lines are obvious: salt crust around walls, rust tongues below conduit brackets, pale mineral lace on locker doors, brown-black bands where microbial film found an air-water boundary and turned it into a living edge. Others are smaller. A label curls only at the bottom. One floor screw blooms green while the rest stay dull. Dust sits too neatly above a shelf because everything below it was rinsed away. A child's scuff line stops at a door because the corridor beyond filled first.

Deep Reach logs called these `historic wetting indicators`. Marauder wall notes use plainer language because plain language keeps the insult in it. The room drowned. Somebody drained it. Somebody decided reopening was cheaper than rebuilding. The wall kept the record when the lease file did not.

Floodlines show behavior, not only damage. A high line with clean lower walls means fast flooding and fast pumping. A low line with thick mineral teeth means slow seepage and long neglect. A line that rises behind equipment but drops near the door means bad slope, blocked drains or a crew that sealed the exit before sealing the leak. A line through an archive cabinet says which records were underwater and which survived by accident.

Read the floodline before the room name.

## 3. False Dryness

The most dangerous wet room is the one that has learned to pass as dry.

False dryness happens when visible water is gone but the reasons for water remain. Pumps can clear a floor while pressure sits in a ceiling blister. Heaters can dry a panel face while insulation behind it stays sour and heavy. Emergency foam can trap brine against cable bundles until copper turns to green meal. A cracked viewport can hold because a mineral skin formed over the wound, then fail when a tool case bumps the frame.

There are paperwork versions too. Black Keel may mark a compartment `dry-accessible` because a drone crossed it once. A buyer may price a recovered tool as clean because its exterior was wiped before custody. A Deep Reach archive may show a successful dewatering cycle and omit that the pump ran on borrowed power from a dead clinic. The words are not always lies. Sometimes they are too small for the room.

Crews distrust clean floors, quiet drains and air pockets with no reason to be alive. A room that admits it is wet is often kinder than a room asking to be trusted.

That is why dye tabs, chalk, humidity thread, cheap pressure stickers and bad jokes survive in every kit. The tools are small because the truth usually starts small.

## 4. Leak Discipline

Leak discipline is the order of operations that keeps a useful room from becoming a grave.

First: stop making the room worse. Do not cut a panel because it is in the way. Do not open a cabinet because it looks dry. Do not cycle a door until someone knows which side has pressure, which side has water and which side has the better lie.

Second: find the source, the path and the future. Water entering from an overhead crack is not the same problem as water climbing through a floor seam. Brine dripping from a cable tray may have travelled twenty meters before choosing that place to fall. A leak under pressure can seal itself with salt and then break when heat, vibration or a careless boot changes the terms. A leak is not a point. It is a route.

Third: mark the truth where the next person can see it. Marauder marks are ugly on purpose: arrows, time, depth, taste if someone risked a sample, pump status, `ceiling heavy`, `do not warm`, `drain talks`, `seal belongs to door not wall`. Pretty labels invite trust. Field marks invite suspicion, which is more useful.

Fourth: repair only what the mission can defend. A patch that holds long enough to retrieve medicine may be good. A patch that invites a crew to sleep under a loaded ceiling is murder with better handwriting. Deep Reach loved permanent language. Marauders prefer expiry times.

The best leak repair says what it is not strong enough to do.

## 5. Water As Evidence

Water is a witness that ruins most paperwork.

It moves dust, floats bodies, stains priority, eats signatures, preserves footprints in silt and deletes them with the next pressure cough. It can prove a door was open before a breach. It can prove a crate was moved after a room flooded. It can show that a claimed sealed route was used by someone with wet boots and no right to be there.

Black Keel values water marks when they support custody and discounts them when the stain lowers margin. Buyers prefer clean salvage because clean objects tell fewer stories. Courts prefer clean chains because water turns sequence into argument. HECTON-8 keeps making wet arguments anyway.

A mineral line on a safe can show when it was opened. A dried runnel across a floor can point to a hidden service void. A wet handle can betray another crew. A dry patch under a body can say the body was moved. A floodline through a memorial wall can make an old official version impossible.

Some crews erase water marks. Better crews copy them first.

## 6. Door, Floor, Ceiling

A leak changes the order of a room.

Doors are not only exits. A door can be the strongest seal in the room, the weakest seal, or the thing holding a pressure pocket in place by accident. If the hinge is warm, the water behind it has work to do. If the handle is dry but the lower gasket tastes of salt, the door has been wiped, not trusted. If the threshold has grit on the dry side, something crossed after the water left.

Floors lie because gravity makes them look honest. A dry floor can cover water under panels, silt in conduits, swollen insulation, charged puddles under gratings and a drain that only works while a dying pump steals power from another room. Step where the floor has reason to hold. Do not step where it has only shine.

Ceilings kill patient people. Trays hold brine above the eyes. Cable bundles sag before they drip. Emergency membranes bulge in corners where nobody looks until the lamp hits them. The first warning may be a screw head sweating or a dust line bending. Never warm a ceiling you have not read.

## 7. Price Of A Dry Room

A dry room is valuable because it can be used. It is dangerous for the same reason.

A dry locker can store medicine, clean seals, route tags, batteries, witness tubes or a worker's last tool roll. Corridors are worth more: air moves, a hose stretches, a return path hides. Paper pays best: an archive that stayed dry turns rumor into custody, and custody is what Black Keel will lift.

Black Keel prices dryness like an asset. Deep Reach priced it like compliance. Marauders price it by time: how long the floor stays useful, how much air it costs, what it hides, and whether the lie will break before the job is done.

Do not buy a room because it is dry. Buy the reason it is still dry, or leave before the reason changes.

## 8. Field Rule

Before you drain, photograph the line.

Before you seal, find the route.

Before you trust a dry floor, ask what is paying for the dryness.

Water does not lose to walls on HECTON-8. It loses time to competent habits. Every floodline is a previous failure that still knows the room better than the form.
