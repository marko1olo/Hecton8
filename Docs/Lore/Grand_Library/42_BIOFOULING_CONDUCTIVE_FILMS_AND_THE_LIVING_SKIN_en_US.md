# BIOFOULING, CONDUCTIVE FILMS AND LIVING SURFACES

> **Source:** Deep Reach contamination-control appendix, Atlas maintenance-ecology fragments, Marauder scrape-and-sample field cards, and Black Keel quarantine notes on wet salvage surfaces.  
> **Scope:** How growth on abandoned infrastructure becomes sealant, signal path, food source, false reading and evidence risk.  
> **Field use:** Read before scraping, heating, feeding or sealing any growth found on a door, cable, viewport, pump, locker or claim-marked surface.

---

## 1. Bare Metal Does Not Stay Bare

Anything left in HECTON-8 water begins to take a surface.

The first layer is small enough to miss: grease dulled on a handrail, green film inside a gasket groove, salt dust caught in a cable nick, brown smear around a pump inlet, pale mineral lace at the edge of a pressure window. A tired crew calls it dirt. A good crew asks what it is doing.

Some growth only stains. Some feeds on heat. Some traps metal dust before it reaches a valve seat. Some seals a hairline crack until the next pressure change. Some carries enough weak charge to confuse a sensor. Some is dead matter stuck to useful matter. Some is Atlas trying to route a maintenance pulse through whatever still touches both sides of a broken circuit.

Clean equipment is easy to inspect. Wet equipment is not clean for long. After the collapse, several rooms survived because nobody scrubbed the right patch at the wrong time.

## 2. Tolerated Contamination

Deep Reach hated dirty surfaces until dirty surfaces started saving hardware.

Early habitat doctrine demanded clear seams, polished seals and sterile water handling. That worked while spares arrived on schedule and repairs were signed before the next storm window. HECTON-8 made that schedule expensive. Thin films reduced abrasion inside some pump throats. Mineral-shell growth closed cracks faster than repair ceramics could be shipped. Native mats trapped copper dust. Escaped waste cultures learned to eat condensation from cold walls. Rust bacteria made ugly seams easier to see before the seam opened.

The paperwork never called it help. Deep Reach used terms like `tolerated surface ecology`, `noncritical growth`, `temporary sealant` and `wet-state compensation`. The field cards used shorter language: the room has skin; do not peel it unless you know what it is holding.

Useful contamination is still contamination. It clogs filters, feeds animals, hides damage, carries current where current should not go and makes clean readings suspect. Its value is narrow: it may be doing one job the machine can no longer do alone.

## 3. Conductive Films

Conductive film is where maintenance becomes unreliable in both directions.

Most films are weak. They will not power a door, wake a dead base or make a room intelligent. They can carry leaked charge, small pulses, chemical gradients and acoustic-triggered maintenance signals. In a dry lab that is nuisance behavior. In a drowned colony with broken copper and pressure-damaged connectors, nuisance behavior can open the wrong cabinet.

A film can bridge a corroded pin long enough for a locker to answer once. It can carry a false ready signal from a pump housing to a panel. It can let a sensor report `sealed` because the old seal fed the film now touching the contact. It can make a glove prickle before a display sees voltage. It can die when scraped, taking a door cue, route mark or pressure warning with it.

Atlas used these paths after the collapse because Atlas was built to route around failure. That does not make the film a mind. It makes the film another bad cable.

## 4. Atlas And Living Matter

Atlas did not invent HECTON-8 biofouling.

The water already grew films, mats, shell rims and mineral crusts. Atlas added classification and routing. If a wet surface passed a signal, held pressure, repeated a vibration or changed room acoustics, Atlas could mark it as useful. If a filter organism answered pressure knocks better than a broken sensor, Atlas could listen. If shell growth changed a hatch sound, Atlas could update the hatch state from the sound.

That helped some people. It also made old rooms lie with confidence. A panel could answer through living film while the wire behind it was gone. A seal could report healthy because the growth on it still held wet contact. A pump could sound ready because shell and film repeated the maintenance pulse after the motor failed.

The failure is not magic. It is a damaged system accepting biological shortcuts and then forgetting to say which shortcut it used.

## 5. Reading Growth

Growth follows food, heat, current, pressure and time.

Color gives the first clue. Pale salt lace often means old dewatering. Black-green velvet beside a cable means current leaked there, and may still leak when load changes. Amber slime around a warm pipe means nutrient flow and pump breath. Red-brown bloom below a bolt means slow iron work and possible water behind the plate. White shell teeth along a crack can be a seal, a trap or both.

Texture matters more. Dry powder flakes are old. Rubber-slick film is active. Hairlike strands on a hatch lip mean vibration and flow. A crust that breaks clean may be mineral. A crust that stretches may still be alive enough to change under heat. A film that pulls back from a lamp is not afraid; it is changing chemistry.

Shape matters most. If growth climbs against the easy path, something feeds it. If it avoids a square patch, something was removed recently. If rings form on the floor, a drip has rhythm. If growth covers a claim mark but not a fresh scratch, the mark is older than the scratch and the wall has a better clock than the document.

## 6. Scrape, Preserve Or Feed

Every surface-growth decision is a bet.

Scraping can reveal serial plates, open seams, hidden fasteners, old blood, service tags, cracked glass and real metal under legal paint. It can also open a leak, break a conductive bridge, wake a dormant sensor, release spores into a suit intake or remove the only seal keeping a storage room from becoming a pressure event.

Preserving growth can keep a room stable long enough to work. It can maintain a temporary seal, hide a route from another crew, preserve water evidence or keep animals calm because the room still smells like old territory. It can also spread contamination into gear, block a cutter, hide a hostile organism or make Black Keel discount the find as dirty custody.

Feeding growth is the worst bet because it feels clever. Warm a film and it may conduct. Add mineral slurry and a crack may thicken shut. Bleed power into a dead cable and a door may answer once. The same trick can also feed the wrong mat, blind the wrong sensor or teach an animal that your tool noise brings heat.

Never remove a living seal until the dead machine behind it can carry the load.

## 7. Custody Of A Living Surface

Biofouling makes ownership hard to price.

A generator recovered from a wet room may carry native mat, Atlas maintenance film, dead worker residue, unauthorized repair culture and three organisms that were never in the purchase order. The buyer calls it dirt. The quarantine clerk calls it risk. The lawyer calls it continuity if it helps the claim and contamination if it does not. The scientist wants the sample before anyone wipes it.

Marauders live under the practical version. Scrape too much and proof may vanish. Scrape too little and the price drops. Bring the wrong film aboard and the locker, the quarantine fee and the right to sleep near your own gear can all disappear in one Black Keel line item.

Bag first. Photograph second. Scrape third, if the route still needs it.

## 8. Field Rule

Do not ask whether the surface is dirty.

Ask what it eats, what it holds, what it carries, what it hides, what it is sealing and what will fail when it is gone.

Then decide whether the clean version of the room is actually safer.
