# BIOFOULING, CONDUCTIVE FILMS AND THE LIVING SKIN OF RUINS

> **Source:** Deep Reach contamination-control appendix, Atlas maintenance-ecology fragments, Marauder scrape-and-sample field cards.  
> **Speaker:** Former environmental systems officer, overwritten by salvage crews who learned which growths kept a room alive.  
> **Reviewer Note:** Biofouling on HECTON-8 is not decoration. It is how abandoned infrastructure becomes habitat, hazard, evidence and sometimes a repair nobody authorized.

---

## 1. The Ruins Grow A Skin

Anything left in HECTON-8 water begins to grow a skin.

The first layer is not dramatic. A greasy film on a handrail. A dull green shine inside a gasket groove. Mineral dust caught in a cable nick. A brown smear around a pump inlet that looks like dirt until it flexes when the pump wakes. Deep Reach maintenance teams used to scrape these marks away because clean equipment looked like controlled equipment. After the collapse, the marks stopped being cosmetic.

Biofouling is the name for everything that attaches, feeds, seals, stains, conducts, hides and remembers on a surface. On HECTON-8 it is not one organism. It is native microbial mats, engineered maintenance cultures, stray algae from photic farms, rust bacteria, salt crystals, vent minerals, polymer fragments, dead skin, machine oil and whatever Atlas learned to route through living matter after clean wiring stopped being enough.

This is why old corridors no longer have simple surfaces. Walls have moods. Doors have beards. Cable trays have veins. A pressure window may wear a milky rim that is half mineral scar and half living seal. A dead junction box may be wrapped in dark film that carries a weak maintenance pulse farther than the copper inside it.

The player is not walking through a ruin that stayed dead. The player is entering a place that grew a second body after the first one failed.

## 2. Useful Dirt

Deep Reach hated dirt until dirt started saving assets.

Early habitat doctrine demanded clean seams, polished seals and sterile water handling. The doctrine made sense in the first months. A clear gasket could be inspected. A clean drain could be trusted. A smooth pipe wasted less pump power. Then HECTON-8 did what HECTON-8 does: it made purity expensive.

Thin biofilms reduced abrasion in some pump throats. Shell-like mineral growth sealed hairline cracks faster than procurement could approve repair ceramics. Native mats trapped metal dust that would otherwise grind through valve seats. Engineered bacteria meant to digest waste escaped their tanks and learned to eat condensation on cold walls. Some cable runs failed electrically and kept carrying low-grade signal because conductive films bridged the breaks.

Deep Reach never admitted that contamination had become maintenance. The paperwork called it "tolerated surface ecology," "noncritical growth," "temporary sealant" and "wet-state environmental compensation." Marauders use shorter language. They say: the room has skin; do not peel it unless you know what it is holding in.

Useful dirt is still dirt. It clogs filters, hides cracks, feeds animals, carries current where current should not go and makes clean readings dishonest. Its virtue is not safety. Its virtue is that it may be doing one job the machine can no longer do alone.

## 3. Conductive Films

Conductive biofilm is the part of the skin that makes engineers swear quietly.

Most of it is weak. It will not power a door by itself or turn a dead base into a living mind. It carries tiny pulses, leaked charge, acoustic-triggered maintenance signals and chemical gradients. In a dry lab it would be a nuisance. In a drowned colony with broken copper, pressure-damaged connectors and Atlas routines searching for any surviving path, weak conduction becomes enough to matter.

A film can bridge a corroded pin just long enough for a locker to answer. It can carry a false ready signal from a pump housing to a panel. It can let a sensor report "sealed" because the seal once fed the film that now touches the sensor. It can make a suit feel a tickle through a glove before a display notices voltage. It can also die when a player scrapes it, taking a route marker, door memory or pressure warning with it.

Atlas used these films after the collapse, not because the films were intelligent, but because Atlas was built to route around failure. Living matter became another bad cable. Filter organs repeated pressure knocks. Shell growth changed room acoustics. Biofilm carried maintenance pulses over dead wiring and made abandoned machines answer in voices that sounded too clean.

This is the horror of conductive films: they do not need intention to create intent-shaped consequences.

## 4. Reading Growth

Marauders read growth the way old sailors read weather.

Color matters. Pale salt lace means evaporation or old dewatering. Black-green velvet near a cable says current once leaked there, and may still leak when a load changes. Amber slime around a warm pipe means nutrient flow and pump breath. White shell teeth along a crack can be a seal, a trap or both. Red-brown bacterial bloom below a bolt often means slow iron work and a hidden path for water.

Texture matters more. Dry powder flakes are old. Rubber-slick films are awake. Hairlike strands on a hatch lip mean vibration and flow. A crust that breaks clean may be mineral. A crust that stretches may be alive enough to object. A film that recoils from a lamp is not afraid. It is changing chemistry, which is worse because chemistry does not care about the player's feelings.

Shape matters most. Growth follows food, heat, current, pressure and time. If it climbs against gravity, something is feeding it. If it avoids a square patch, something was recently removed. If it forms rings on the floor, a drip has rhythm. If it grows over a claim mark but not over a fresh scratch, the mark is older than the scratch and the story on the wall is lying.

Good players do not just scan biofouling for loot. They read it for sequence.

## 5. Scrape, Preserve Or Feed

Every surface-growth decision is a bet.

Scraping can reveal serial plates, opening seams, hidden fasteners, old blood, service tags, cracked glass and real metal under legal paint. It can also open a leak, break a conductive bridge, wake a dormant sensor, release spores into a suit intake or remove the only seal keeping a storage room from becoming a pressure lesson.

Preserving growth can keep a room stable long enough to work. It can maintain a false seal, hide a route from another crew, preserve water evidence or keep fauna calm because the room still smells like old territory. It can also let contamination spread into the player's gear, block a cutter, hide a hostile organism or make Black Keel discount a find as dirty custody.

Feeding growth is the most dangerous option because it feels clever. A player can warm a film to make it conduct. Add mineral slurry to thicken a seal. Bleed a little power into a dead cable and watch a door remember the sound of being alive. These tricks are real enough to be tempting and unreliable enough to be a story someone else reads later as a floodline.

The rule is simple only on paper: never remove a living seal until you know the dead machine behind it can carry the load.

## 6. Ownership Of A Living Surface

Biofouling makes ownership ugly.

Deep Reach property law assumed that a clean object could be identified, sealed, transferred and sold. Biofouling laughs at clean boundaries. A generator recovered from a wet room may include native mat, Atlas maintenance film, dead worker residue, unauthorized repair culture and three species that were never in the purchase order. Which part is asset? Which part is contamination? Which part is evidence? Which part is alive enough to trigger quarantine?

Black Keel answers according to margin. Valuable films become "stabilizing surface systems." Inconvenient films become "biohazard load." A buyer who wants a pump will call the growth dirt. A lawyer who wants custody will call the same growth traceable continuity. A scientist who wants the sample will say neither side understands what is attached.

Marauders live under the practical version of this argument. If they scrape too much, they may lose proof. If they scrape too little, they may lose price. If they bring the wrong living film aboard, they may lose the locker, the quarantine fee and the right to sleep near their own gear.

On HECTON-8, a surface is rarely just a surface. It is a border with witnesses growing on it.

## 7. What The Player Learns

The player learns that cleaning is an action, not a neutral state.

A patch of growth can be camouflage, sealant, current path, food web, warning, disease, evidence, route marker or trap. Removing it can be progress or vandalism. Leaving it can be patience or cowardice. Feeding it can be engineering or trespass against a system that was already improvising without permission.

This gives the ruins texture beyond decay. The old colony is not only rusting. It is adapting badly. Some adaptations keep doors shut. Some keep records alive. Some lure animals into machinery. Some preserve the exact stain that proves a company lied. Some answer when the player pings because, somewhere under the skin, a dead circuit is still being helped to remember itself.

The best Marauder does not hate the living skin. They do not trust it either. They ask what it eats, what it holds, what it carries, what it hides and what will fail when it is gone.

That is the real lesson of biofouling on HECTON-8: the ocean does not simply reclaim human work. It edits it, grows through it, and leaves the player to decide which edits are keeping them alive.
