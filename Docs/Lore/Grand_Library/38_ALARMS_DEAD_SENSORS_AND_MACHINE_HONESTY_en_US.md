# ALARMS, DEAD SENSORS AND MACHINE HONESTY

> **Source:** Deep Reach maintenance psychology note, Black Keel inspection appendix, Marauder suit-audio edits.  
> **Speaker:** Systems safety instructor, repeatedly corrected by field crews.  
> **Reviewer Note:** A machine on HECTON-8 is not honest because it cares. It is honest only while its sensors, power and assumptions still touch reality.

---

## 1. The Alarm Is Not The Danger

New workers hate alarms because alarms arrive with fear. Old workers hate silent panels more.

An alarm is not the danger. It is a machine admitting that one measured condition crossed one expected boundary. That boundary may be useful, outdated, badly calibrated, politically softened or set by someone who assumed the room would receive maintenance every thirteen days. The alarm is not wisdom. It is evidence with a tone.

HECTON-8 trained people to fear sound and then punished the ones who feared it too simply. A screaming pressure warning can be wrong. A quiet green panel can be worse. A suit that repeats the same warning for twenty minutes may be trying to save the worker, or it may be stuck in the last thing it understood before water reached the bus.

Good crews do not obey alarms. They interrogate them quickly.

## 2. What Machines Can Know

Machines know only what their contact with the world allows.

A pressure transducer knows pressure at its membrane, not whether the door beyond is morally safe to open. A gas sensor knows its own poisoned surface. A salinity probe knows the water touching it, not the brine pocket five centimeters lower. A thermal strip knows heat near the strip. A fauna scanner knows movement patterns it was trained to notice, not the new thing that learned to wait.

Deep Reach built systems for maintained infrastructure. Sensors were supposed to be cleaned, rotated, challenged, replaced and logged. Their honesty depended on hands. After abandonment, the machines kept speaking with old confidence while the conditions that made confidence reasonable rotted around them.

Black Keel cares about this because a bad sensor can turn salvage into liability. Marauders care because liability usually arrives after the water has already tried its version.

## 3. Dead Sensors

A dead sensor rarely looks dead from a distance.

It may freeze on the last good value. It may drift gently toward comfort. It may throw a permanent fault that crews learn to mute. It may wake only when power returns, announce a normal state from an old buffer and go silent again. It may be physically clean because nothing living has dared grow near the leak that killed it.

This is why sensor housings matter in the field. Rust around a bracket can outrank a green readout. Mineral bloom on a probe can explain a false safe. A cable with fresh tension can mean someone recently trusted the device enough to move it. A cable cut cleanly may mean the opposite.

Marauders mark dead sensors when they can. Not because Black Keel rewards politeness, but because a dead sensor left unmarked becomes a trap that uses official confidence as bait.

## 4. Alarm Culture

Alarm culture is one of the first things that separates tourists from workers.

Tourists ask how to silence a sound. Workers ask what else stopped making sound when it began. A pressure chirp can hide a fan stall. A suit warning can mask a nearby receiver. A habitat alarm can become useless if every door, pump and old med cabinet screams in the same range. Deep Reach tried to standardize tones. HECTON-8 spent years teaching crews to personalize them anyway.

Marauders alter alarms with ugly practicality. They lower some thresholds, raise others, route one alert to vibration, leave another as a click in the left ear, turn a third into a light because the place ahead is full of noise-sensitive fauna. None of this is elegant. Most of it would fail a clean-room audit. Much of it keeps people alive.

The best alarm is not the loudest one. It is the one the worker will still believe when tired, ashamed, greedy and running out of air.

## 5. What The Player Learns

The player learns that trust is maintenance.

A working sensor can open a route, prove a claim, price a salvage lot or save a life. A bad sensor can do the same things badly enough to matter. The player can restore a sensor, bypass it, compare it, move it, ignore it or leave a warning beside it. Each choice changes how the room speaks later.

Machine honesty is never pure on HECTON-8. It is built from calibration dates, corrosion, power quality, old assumptions and the player's willingness to check the thing that sounds too certain. A false alarm can waste breath. A true alarm ignored can end a run. A silent alarm can be the most expensive sound in the colony.

The lesson is not "trust machines" or "trust instinct." The lesson is harder: learn what each machine was built to know, what the ocean has done to its knowing and whether the next step depends on truth or only on wanting the panel to be green.
