# ALARMS, DEAD SENSORS AND MACHINE HONESTY

> **Source:** Deep Reach alarm-response cards, sensor calibration ledgers, Black Keel inspection appendix, and Marauder suit-audio cuts from reopened maintenance decks.  
> **Scope:** Alarm trust, dead sensors, stale buffers, bad thresholds, field verification and machine records used for route decisions, salvage claims and survival work.  
> **Field use:** Read before muting an alarm, trusting a green panel, opening a sealed room from remote status, moving a probe, or using a sensor log as proof.

---

## 1. A Panel Repeats What Reaches It

An alarm on HECTON-8 is not the danger. It is a statement from a chain.

Something in the room touches pressure, heat, gas, salinity, vibration, current, hinge position, pump flow or animal motion. A sensor turns that contact into a value. A controller compares the value with a threshold. A panel, suit or speaker turns the comparison into a sound, color, haptic tick or logged event. Every link can be honest. Every link can also be late, wet, starved, patched, misnamed or powered from somewhere nobody wrote down.

This is why old workers do not ask whether an alarm is true. They ask what reached it.

A pressure alarm can be true at the membrane and useless at the door. A clear air reading can be true at the ceiling and lethal near the sump. A green hatch status can mean the latch is closed, the latch sensor is closed, the latch sensor is stuck closed, or the controller woke from a buffer that was last valid before the annex flooded. The panel does not know which of those histories is morally important. It repeats what arrived.

Deep Reach taught calm response, not blind obedience. Black Keel later edited the cards to protect claims. Marauders cut the cards shorter because breath is expensive. The useful version is still the same: do not worship the panel, and do not sneer at it. Find the chain.

## 2. Truth Starts At The Sensing Surface

Machine honesty begins where the machine touches the world.

A pressure transducer knows pressure at its membrane. Salt film on that membrane changes what truth can reach it. A gas sensor knows the chemistry on its poisoned surface, not the air behind a closed cabinet. A salinity probe knows the water touching its pins, not the denser brine lying five centimeters lower. A thermal strip knows the metal under its adhesive. A camera sees what the lens has not grown over. A fauna scanner recognizes motion patterns it was trained to mark, not the patient animal that learned to wait.

The colony was built around maintained contact. Probes were cleaned, challenged against known samples, rotated out before drift became embarrassing, and logged with a technician's name. That procedure was not paperwork. It was part of the sensor. Without it, the same instrument keeps speaking in the tone of a maintained machine while its contact with reality decays.

HECTON-8 punishes clean-room thinking here. A perfect readout from a sealed box may be less useful than a dirty bracket, a cable bend, a mineral bloom or the smell of hot insulation. Field crews read the instrument and the body of the instrument. The stain around the screw can be more current than the screen.

## 3. The Ways A Sensor Lies

Most dead sensors do not announce death.

Some freeze on the last good value. These are the polite killers. A corridor remains "dry" forever because the water arrived after the bus died. A freezer remains "cold" because the temperature packet never aged out. A door remains "sealed" because the latch reed broke in the safe position.

Some drift toward comfort. A pressure sensor with a fouled membrane slowly learns the pressure everyone wants to see. A gas cell near the end of its life stops smelling the thing it was bought to fear. A salinity probe crusted in mineral deposits reports a softened sea while brine pools under the grating.

Some fail loud. These get muted first. After three weeks of false shrieking, crews stop hearing the fourth week. Then a real leak chooses the same channel.

Some fail by being fed badly. Brownout power can make a controller reboot into old thresholds. Borrowed power can keep a warning lamp alive after the sensor rail has died. A damaged ground can make every vibration sensor in a corridor agree with a pump that is no longer turning. Machines do not need malice to lie. They only need a broken path from the world to the record.

## 4. Alarm Culture

Alarm culture separates station tourists from people who worked below the safe lines.

Tourists ask how to silence a sound. Workers ask what else changed when the sound began. Did the fan note drop before the gas chirp? Did the suit haptic start before the wall speaker? Did the panel go amber after the relay clicked, or did the relay click because the panel lost voltage? Was the alarm repeated by an independent system, or only echoed through the same wet bus?

Deep Reach tried to standardize tones by class and urgency. HECTON-8 forced exceptions into every deck. Some alarms became vibration because noise-sensitive fauna nested near the route. Some became a left-ear click because suit radios already carried too much speech. Some became dim light because darkness made people slow and sound made predators fast. Clean auditors called this inconsistency. Working crews called it survival.

The dangerous alarm is not always loud. Loud alarms earn attention and resentment. Quiet alarms can sit beneath fatigue until the room has already decided the price. The best alarm is one a tired worker will still believe after shame, greed, argument and low oxygen have started editing judgment.

## 5. Dead Sensors As Route Hazards

A dead sensor can hold a route shut. A worse one can open it.

Remote status is cheap, so it lies cheaply. A locked annex can look clear because every interior probe died on the same clean number, and a flooded corridor can look blocked because a pressure transducer still reports a two-day-old surge. Failure direction picks the lie: a spoilage sensor that failed dangerous keeps a medical cabinet shut, while a chilled-chain alarm that died first opens a specimen vault on green, contents spoiled.

Route crews treat dead sensors as physical objects, not abstract warnings. They tag housings, photograph cable damage, leave scrape marks near bypassed panels and write short notes where the next crew will actually look. "Bad gas read at ankle height." "Door green lies when pump two is off." "Do not trust camera; lens skin moves." None of that is elegant. It is how a ruin becomes legible without becoming safe.

Black Keel pays for sensor states when those states affect value. A working humidity log can prove a crate remained sellable. A bad seal sensor can explain why a hatch was cut instead of opened. A silent biohazard alarm can make a profitable locker into evidence. The same dead instrument that nearly kills a worker can become the difference between salvage and theft if its failure is recorded well enough.

## 6. Machine Records Are Claims

On HECTON-8, logs are not neutral. They are claims with timestamps.

Deep Reach wrote logs to protect operations. Black Keel reads them to assign ownership, liability and price. Marauders read them to learn which door killed the last team, which pump still drinks power, which locker is worth a battery, and which alarm was silenced by someone who expected to return.

That makes sensor truth political. A stale buffer can make an annex look untouched. A manual bypass can make a legal entry look like forced intrusion. A reset alarm counter can erase the hour when a sample warmed. A clean green snapshot can sell a route to a weaker crew. The machine did not negotiate the price, but the record it kept may decide who gets paid and who gets blamed.

Good crews therefore preserve bad news. They keep screenshots of ugly panels. They record contradictory readings. They mark uncertainty instead of polishing it away. A clean claim built from a dirty ruin usually needs one careful sentence: "Sensor state unreliable after waterline; physical inspection supersedes panel." That sentence has saved more oxygen, money and blood than many louder warnings.

## 7. Field Rules

Trust a sensor only after identifying what it touches, how it is powered and when it last had reason to know the truth.

Compare unlike instruments when the next step is irreversible. Pressure plus door frame. Gas plus airflow. Salinity plus floor stain. Temperature plus condensation. Camera plus scraped lens housing. A second copy of the same bad assumption is not confirmation.

Treat silence as a reading. If the room should be screaming and is not, find out what lost voice. A muted channel, dead rail, cut speaker or frozen buffer can be more important than the alarm that still works.

Mark every bypass that changes future meaning. If a panel stays green only because a probe was unplugged, the next crew needs that fact more than they need confidence.

Do not shame caution into quiet. HECTON-8 is full of rooms where the first honest machine was annoying and the last calm panel was lying. The work is not to trust machines or distrust them. The work is to keep the chain between world, sensor, record and hand short enough that a living person can still judge it.
