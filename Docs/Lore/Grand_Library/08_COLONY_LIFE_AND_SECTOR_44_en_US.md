# SECTOR 44 BEFORE THE WATER

> **Source:** Recovered living-quarter boards, canteen ledgers, route-permission slips and private notes from Sector 44.  
> **Compiler:** Barnard Yards public archive packet, Aegir Claim shelf file 44-LQ.  
> **Spoiler level:** Early-to-midgame colony context. No final payload outcomes.

Sector 44 did not look haunted while people were alive in it.

It looked overused. The bunks had pressure tape on the lockers because the latches rattled during pump restart. The canteen kept a handwritten water ledger even after Deep Reach installed a clean dispenser panel, because the panel counted allowance and the workers counted trust. The tool board outside Bay 3 had three empty hooks marked for clamp gauges; one card says they were on loan to pump crew, another says they were never returned from Annex P-63, and someone wrote in grease pencil: "Ask Mara. She knows which lie is cheaper."

That is the colony before the Great Tide: not a doomed temple, not a monster story, not a corporate brochure. A pressure town with bad sleep, tight margins, working jokes and too many systems that were technically still within tolerance.

## A Working Day

Most shifts began with the sound of air moving through wet filters. Sector 44 workers learned the station by cadence: the lift fan at 05:40, the pump surge at 06:00, the relay check at 06:15, the canteen heat cycle at 06:20. New staff thought the schedule was discipline. Old staff knew it was a diagnostic. If the fan came late, the east corridor had standing water. If the pump came hard, a sump sensor had lied. If the canteen heat cycle skipped, somebody in power routing had borrowed the morning comfort load for a repair that would not appear on the welfare board.

Deep Reach called the routine "distributed resilience." Workers called it counting what the station was not saying.

Nobody had a single job. A hydroponics tech could be pulled into seal cleaning. A cutter tech could spend half a shift carrying brine samples because the drone rack was in maintenance hold. A route notary could become a witness for a pressure-door restart, because two signatures made a bad order look safe enough to file. The colony survived by overlapping skills, borrowed tools and informal warnings that never reached the official handbook.

The official board outside Sector 44 listed task categories: habitat upkeep, cable reef support, pump maintenance, sample handling, relief work, quarantine cleaning. The private board underneath listed the real categories: dry socks, clean gasket, full tank, no solo return, do not trust the north gauge after dinner.

## Water Accounting

Water was everywhere and still rationed.

This is the first thing surface readers get wrong. HECTON-8 did not lack water. It lacked water that was in the right place, at the right pressure, with the right chemistry, inside a pipe that had not been taught by the ocean to lie.

The canteen ledger is one of the best surviving records because it was too ordinary to redact. Each line marks worker ID, shift, drinking allowance, wash allowance, medical exception and debt adjustment. Some pages are clean. Some are crowded with corrections: "heat rash," "sealant burn," "brine exposure," "returned late from Kestrel window," "took Venn's share, approved by Venn." There are no speeches in it. Just people moving water from a shared account into bodies that had to go back outside.

The dispenser panel kept better numbers. The ledger kept better truth.

Mara Venn appears in the water book more often than in formal rosters. Her job title was pump-room lead, but her mark shows up beside borrowed wash minutes, extra electrolyte packets and one denied penalty. A later Deep Reach summary calls those entries "informal welfare drift." The handwriting makes it look less abstract. Someone was trying to keep a crew upright without making the station admit the crew was failing.

## Food, Light And Small Luxury

Sector 44 ate what could be grown, cultured, packed or justified through cargo mass.

The common joke was that every meal had three flavors: protein, iodine and invoice. Vat cultures filled most trays. Filter kelp and shelf algae were safe only after lab clearance. Brine-grown supplements arrived in sealed strips that tasted metallic when the humidity rose. Fresh fruit was not a lost Earth memory here; many workers were not from Earth at all. It was a route luxury, something that came through Barnard, Luyten, Aegir transfer, claim custody and somebody's mass allowance.

The hydroponics lights were not there to imitate home. They were there to keep crops predictable and workers less broken during long interior stretches. When the lights failed, people did not write poetry about darkness. They got headaches, argued over sleep, misread warning colors and made stupid mistakes with hot tools.

Small luxury mattered because the ocean removed privacy first. A dry sleeve. A clean towel. A working toothbrush head. Two minutes in a corner where the pumps were muffled by storage crates. A printed photo sealed in laminate, not because it was sentimental by itself, but because laminate meant the person expected it to survive condensation.

The archive recovered sixteen personal images from Sector 44 lockers. Only six show faces. The others show tools, a dry bunk, a coil of cable tied neatly, a bowl of shelf greens before inspection, a view of Aegir on a clear transfer day. People kept proof that order had existed.

## Sound Discipline

There was no single named pressure madness in Sector 44. Later gossip and bad claim journalism tried to compress it into one phrase.

There was sleep debt. There were pressure headaches, bad scrubber weeks, alarm fatigue, isolation, debt mail, delayed replies and acoustic contamination from pumps, hull stress and exterior work. There were people who heard patterns that were not useful and people who ignored patterns that would have saved them. The line between those mistakes was not moral. It was often six months of poor sleep and a gauge nobody could get replaced.

Crew rules grew around sound. Do not tap a pipe as a joke. Do not whistle near the relay wall. Do not run a cutter test during meal heat. Knock twice before cycling a bunkroom hatch, because one knock could be a pressure tick and three meant a pump call. Speak low during a restart. Let the room finish listening before you ask it to work.

Some of that sounds superstitious after the fact. In the station it was maintenance culture. Sound was cheap telemetry. Sound was also the first thing stress turned into a ghost.

Juno Kade's relay notes preserve the better version of it: "If the wall says a name, check the packet queue before you check your head. If the packet queue is clean, check the fan. If the fan is clean, sleep. In that order."

## Tools And Permission

The colony's social map followed tools more than corridors.

A person with a rated clamp gauge could open routes another person could only stare at. A sealant patch gun with fresh cartridges made its holder briefly popular. A cutter contact could be worth more than a week's comfort allowance if it was certified for pressure work instead of scrap trimming. Sector 44's tool board reads like a family tree written by exhausted mechanics: names crossed out, hooks reassigned, tape marks, penalty notes, repair promises.

Tool certification was supposed to keep people alive. It also kept people slow.

Deep Reach loved permissions because permissions made responsibility legible. Every door cycle, every clamp issue, every pressure-rated repair and every sample handoff could be filed. The filing mattered. If a seal failed after an uncertified repair, the worker died and the ledger stayed clean. If a certified repair failed, the claim had a body attached.

That is why workers forged small permissions and feared large ones. A false lunch mark was nothing. A false seal authorization could become a death that accounting understood better than grief.

Sahana Iqbal's triage tags show the cost. She patched burns, pressure bruises, cracked nail beds, chemical cough and eye damage from bad mask seals. Many tags end with the same instruction: "Fit for interior duty only." Many route boards then sent the same person outside.

## Quotas Without Villain Speeches

Nobody needed to stand in a room and announce that people mattered less than material. The station did it through ordinary choices.

When sample custody rose, comfort loads dropped first. When a brine run returned late, the canteen lost heat before the substrate vault lost stabilization. When a relay window opened, sleep shifted around it. When storm forecasts threatened access, maintenance tasks became "deferable" unless they protected extraction, communication or custody. Each choice could be defended. Each choice could be printed in clean language.

Deep Reach did not have to say "suffocate them." It could say: conserve. Delay. Reclassify. Hold for review. Maintain continuity. Protect viable substrate. Reduce noncritical draw. Await external authorization.

Sector 44 is useful because it shows the crime before the crime. The disaster chain did not begin with a single evil order. It began with a town trained to accept small degradations as weather.

Ren Okoye's brine maps carry the same warning in field language: "A safe route is not safe after three budget meetings. Check the wall, not the file."

## The Last Normal Day

The last normal day in Sector 44 was not fully normal. The archive has enough fragments to reconstruct a morning that should have frightened more people than it did.

The east sump logged two false-dry readings. Bay 3 borrowed clamp gauges and did not return them. The canteen heat cycle skipped, then restarted at reduced draw. A worker on interior-only duty was reassigned to a short exterior route because the storm window had narrowed. Lian Torres signed out vent-forge insulation strips for a repair listed as "temporary environmental separation." The phrase appears again after the Tide in a Deep Reach loss conversion file, stripped of the names around it.

At 11:40 station time, someone taped a new note to the private board:

"If the water starts coming from above, do not argue with the panel. Take the west ladder."

The note is practical. That is what makes it difficult to read. It does not predict the Great Tide, name a villain or understand Atlas. It says somebody saw a failure shape and translated it into a route.

The west ladder jammed later. Not because the note was wrong. Because the station needed too many correct things at once.

## What The Player Finds

By 2190, Sector 44 should not feel like a museum of victims. It should feel like a workplace the ocean kept using after the people were gone.

The water ledger tells the player who shared resources. Tool boards tell who had access and who was sent anyway. Triage tags tell which bodies were already compromised before the official emergency. Route slips tell when permissions followed liability instead of safety. Personal images tell what people thought was worth sealing against damp. Marauder corrections can turn all of it into survival information: which gauge lied, which ladder jammed, which seal was temporary, which room was trusted for the wrong reason.

That is the human layer HECTON-8 needs. Not a wall of names and not a clean body count. A set of ordinary systems that still accuse the people who wrote them.
