# PING GHOSTS, QUIET WORK AND LISTENING DISCIPLINE

> **Source:** Deep Reach acoustic navigation primer, receiver maintenance logs, Black Keel receiver-recovery policy, and Marauder suit-audio field notes.  
> **Scope:** Acoustic returns, stale receiver IDs, quiet work habits, and sound records used for routes, claims and risk calls.  
> **Field use:** Read before sending an active ping, trusting an old responder, muting suit audio, salvaging a receiver, or cutting in a room with unknown listeners.

---

## 1. Sound Is Never Private

Under HECTON-8's water, a sound belongs to the route before it belongs to the person who made it.

A dropped wrench can travel through a deck plate, a flooded cable tray, a suit frame and the silt under the next room. A pump start can carry farther through structure than through open water. A cutter that sounds clean in one compartment can sound wrong through a bent bulkhead. Speech is worse: a private joke on suit comms can become a vibration in a receiver mount, and the mount does not know it was meant to be private.

Deep Reach built acoustic navigation because light fails quickly and radio becomes a local habit with a short range. Pings, chirps, coded knocks, tether hums, beacon handshakes and receiver timing made a second map over the colony. That map was never clean. It was useful, which is not the same thing.

Old crews learned to ask two questions before making noise: what answer do we need, and who else gets the question?

## 2. Ping Ghosts

A ping ghost is a return from something that is no longer where the system says it is, or no longer what the system says it is.

The common kind is a stale receiver. A node keeps answering after its bracket twists, its corridor floods, or the wall around it shears away. The identifier is real. The position is not. A suit nav unit sees the ID and wants to believe the old route still exists.

The expensive kind is a shaped return. Folded hull metal can throw back the timing of a passage. Silt can bury a beacon but leave enough antenna exposed to answer late. A dead drone can repeat a charging handshake because the cradle still has power. A collapsed service throat can sound open if the broken rails form the right pocket.

Deep Reach called these acoustic artifacts. Black Keel calls them verification risks. Marauders call them ping ghosts because the word makes people careful without making the phenomenon supernatural. A ghost is stale certainty with a measurable delay.

An answer is not proof of a route. It is proof that something answered.

## 3. Receiver Age

Receivers get old in ways that paperwork hides.

The serial tag can stay legible after the mount rotates ninety degrees. Firmware can keep broadcasting the last good depth because the pressure cell died politely. A maintenance log can show `replaced gasket` when the technician only wiped salt off the connector and billed the part. A beacon can pass a handshake while its physical face points into a collapsed wall.

Black Keel recovery policy treats intact receivers as salvage, navigation value and possible claim evidence. That makes every receiver worth lying about. A crew may drag a node into a better route and keep its old ID. A buyer may prefer a clean timing log over an honest one. A tired worker may reset a fault because the next pump cycle is already late.

The first rule is to separate identity from location. The second rule is to separate location from access. The third rule is to write down which rule you had to break.

## 4. Quiet Work

Quiet work is not romance. It is not the same as stealth. It is the habit of making only the sounds the job can afford.

A careful clamp lands once. A careless clamp talks until someone tapes it. A cutter has a steady pitch when the operator is patient; when the operator rushes, the teeth announce it through the frame. A suit fan under clean load becomes background. A fan chewing salt sounds wet, uneven and expensive. Metal under stress gives warning before it gives way, but only to people who have left room to hear it.

Marauders pad loose hooks, kill unnecessary confirmation chirps, keep voice checks short in wreck throats and leave some clicks alive so a nervous suit does not invent worse signals. A silent site is not the target; silence deletes the warnings with the noise. Each sound is made answerable.

If a noise has no owner, no need and no time limit, it gets removed.

## 5. Listening As Claim Work

Sound records can carry money, liability and guilt.

A pump signature can show that a room was already active before entry. A door motor can prove the lock cycled after a pressure dip. Fauna motion on passive hydrophones can show a route was occupied before a crew disturbed it. A beacon log can show a distress call was alive when Black Keel marked the asset dead.

The same record can burn the crew that keeps it. A receiver log can place a suit inside a restricted route. A muted alarm can make a claim cleaner and a casualty harder to defend. A private audio edit can look like tampering if the inspection officer wants leverage. A clean recording is valuable because it has not yet said who it hurts.

Do not record everything by habit. Do not delete by fear. Keep enough sound to prove sequence, and mark every mute that changed the sequence.

## 6. Noise As Bait

Noise can be a tool, but it is never free.

Active pings can wake old responders, map a void, startle fauna, pull another crew's attention, or tell a hidden receiver someone is looking. A timed knock tests a wall without cutting it; a false beacon pulls a claim jumper off a route. Both bills arrive late: a speaker that pushed a creature back once taught it what a suit sounds like.

Deep Reach manuals separated navigation, deterrence and communication. The colony stopped honoring that separation early. The same chirp can locate a door, prove a log, annoy a predator, call a drone and expose a worker. Context decides which bill arrives first.

Use noise when silence costs more. Use silence when the answer would be more dangerous than the question.

## 7. Field Rule

Before pinging, know what return would change the plan.

Before trusting a ghost, verify identity, location and access as separate facts.

Before muting a sound, mark what the record will no longer prove.

HECTON-8 is not quiet. It is crowded with pumps, receivers, cracked metal, suit frames, animals and old systems still answering late. Listening is work. Sending sound is work. The careless crew does both by reflex and pays for the difference later.
