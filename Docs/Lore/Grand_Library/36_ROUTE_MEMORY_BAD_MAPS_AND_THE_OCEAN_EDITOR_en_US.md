# ROUTE MEMORY, BAD MAPS AND THE OCEAN EDITOR

> **Source:** Black Keel route intake appendix, Deep Reach survey fragments, Marauder path notes.  
> **Speaker:** Navigation instructor, with later salvage annotations.  
> **Reviewer Note:** On HECTON-8, a map is proof that someone survived a path once. It is not a promise that the path still exists.

---

## 1. The Ocean Edits

Dry maps flatter people. They make distance look obedient.

HECTON-8 does not obey like that. Pressure buckles corridors. Brine slips downhill like a patient animal. Silt fills a doorway slowly enough that no alarm calls it a collapse. A service tunnel can remain physically open while becoming unusable because one receiver dies, one gasket blooms, one fauna nest grows across the intake. The path is still there. The route is gone.

Old survey charts are useful because they record intent. They show where Deep Reach thought people should move, where emergency teams were expected to gather, where machines were supposed to breathe heat into the water and where safe rooms were supposed to wait with power. But the ocean edits intent without asking permission. It does not erase everything. It changes enough.

That is why a good route note never says "safe." It says when, by whom, with what suit, under what load, after what storm, before which door failed.

## 2. Route Memory

Routes on HECTON-8 remember through layers.

The official layer is clean: survey lines, access numbers, maintenance schedules, pressure classes, acoustic anchors and drone waypoints. It is the layer most likely to be archived and the least likely to be current. The worker layer is messier: shortcut cuts, handholds polished by repeated gloves, cable ties on low pipes, old chalk sealed under mineral skin. The Marauder layer is rougher still: scrape marks, tether knots, private warnings, a receiver tuned to blink only for a crew that no longer answers.

None of these layers is enough alone. Official data tells the player what the route was for. Worker memory tells how living people actually used it. Marauder memory tells what changed after the colony stopped being maintained. When the three disagree, the disagreement is not noise. It is the clue.

A dead-end on the map may be a living shortcut. A clean corridor may be a trap because nobody has marked it. A route with too many warnings may be the safest path in the district because at least it has been read recently.

## 3. Bad Maps

A bad map is not always wrong. Sometimes it is worse: it is almost right.

Almost-right maps kill professionals. They lead a tired diver to the correct bulkhead through the wrong pressure pocket. They mark a ladder that still exists but ends under silt. They route around a flooded compartment that is no longer flooded, then through a dry locker that is now a brine sump. They preserve old confidence after the facts have moved on.

Map fraud exists, but more often the lie is age. A seller does not need to invent a route if they can sell one before admitting it is stale. A crew does not need malice to leave bad data behind; they only need to survive in a hurry. Deep Reach did not need to be evil for its emergency overlays to become dangerous. It only needed to leave, stop updating them and let water do the rest.

Black Keel grades maps with suspicion. Fresh scans matter. Attachment metadata matters. The pattern of missing notes matters. A map with no corrections is either new, useless or cleaned for sale.

## 4. How Routes Are Read

Experienced Marauders read routes like bodies.

They look for polished corners where shoulders brushed the same turn. They look for silt piled on one side of a threshold, showing which way current has been breathing. They look for cable scars where a drone dragged home on low power. They smell hot insulation through bad filters. They trust a dead receiver less than the rust around its mount. They notice when fauna avoids a perfectly open passage.

This is not mysticism. It is attention under pressure. HECTON-8 rewards the player for learning the small grammar of movement: what repeated contact does to paint, what panic does to a handrail, what a long-abandoned route sounds like when the suit stops filling the silence with warnings.

The best path is rarely the shortest one. It is the one whose lies the player understands.
