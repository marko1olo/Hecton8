# STYX DROP PODS: THE ONE-WAY ARGUMENT

> **Source:** *Black Keel* Descent Operations Manual, Annex D-44, "Contractor Bathy-Drop Delivery."  
> **Speaker:** Annex voice. The delivery-loss clauses are Marek Ibarra's Keelmark Mutual draft, Q4 2147, carried into current issue without rewording.  
> **Issue authority:** Recovery Compliance Office, 2190 return-action cycle. Ibarra's desk closed with the colony. His sentences did not.  
> **Reviewer note:** Public-safe cutaway. This copy describes the pod, not the private claim-window keys, ascent charge signatures, or insurer kill thresholds.

---

## 1. Not A Shuttle

A Styx pod is not a shuttle, lifeboat, elevator, or rescue capsule. It is a disposable bathy-drop shell built to move one suited operator from the *Black Keel* to a legal worksite on HECTON-8 with the least mass that an insurer will accept.

The old manuals call it an "atmosphere-ocean contractor delivery unit." Marauders call it the coffin, the nail, the drop shell, or the first bad deal. Both descriptions are true. The pod is a narrow pressure cylinder wrapped in ablator, ballast, acoustic hardware, impact foam, and enough guidance electronics to lose an argument with weather. It has no pilot station. It has no window. The operator lies locked into a Class-IV suit cradle while the pod handles the part of the transit that would kill a human body before the ocean even gets a chance.

Deep Reach did not design the Styx for comfort. The original pattern was a drill-drop bus used to punch autonomous mining heads through Aegir storm bands. When the colony failed and claim recovery became cheaper than rescue, the drill bus was re-certified for living cargo. The certification did not say "safe." It said "survivable under contracted parameters."

Keelmark's actuarial sheet lists an 88.4 percent survivable delivery rate with weather, pod age, suit seal and operator biometrics all inside green margins. The sheet has no row for a drop where two of those four are amber, which is most of them. It classifies a failed drop as a delivery loss and files it against the shell's pattern number, not against a name.

That classification is older than the practice it governs. Ibarra's desk cut the delivery-loss schedule in the quarter the colony drowned, in the same reconciliation cycle that moved unrecovered colonists into inventory categories, and it was written before a single pod had carried a living contractor down. The office that signs the schedule now is not his. The wording is.

## 2. The Descent Profile

The launch begins in quiet. The pod is clamped inside a rotary cradle on the *Black Keel*. Final checks run faster than a person can read: suit lock, spinal foam, helmet feed, scrubber reserve, ballast valve, uplink mast, acoustic pinger, claim hash, impact clock. The controller does not ask whether the operator is ready. The contract already answered.

Then the cradle throws.

The pod leaves the carrier on a magnetically assisted deorbit path, blunt end forward, ablator heating before the operator feels the first heavy drag. HECTON-8's upper atmosphere is not a clean blue shell. It is storm haze, charged dust, iodine-brown cloud, and static thrown upward from the ocean by Aegir's magnetic weather. The Styx crosses it as a bright wound. The ablator boils away in layers. The pressure frame rings. Every loose screw becomes a bell.

Guidance during this stage is deliberately stupid. The pod does not fly a graceful route. Grace costs fuel and moving parts. It accepts a corridor, burns through turbulence, corrects only when the impact ellipse drifts outside the insured shelf, and treats every other discomfort as an operator problem. A luxury descent would preserve the human. A Styx descent preserves the claim.

Water impact is the second launch. HECTON-8's surface is dense, cold, and moving. The pod strikes at terminal speed with a sacrificial shock skirt deployed around the nose. The skirt breaks, the ablator cracks, the foam cradle crushes around the suit, and the ballast system floods before the pod can bounce. A clean hit leaves the operator concussed, alive, and sinking. A bad hit turns the pressure cylinder sideways, shears the uplink mast, and begins the story with alarms.

The first minutes below the surface are the most honest minutes the machine has. The pod is too loud for lies. Hull cooling ticks through the frame. Ballast valves cough. Water hammers the outer shell. The pinger begins its slow acoustic call to the *Black Keel*: operator present, shell descending, claim still open.

## 3. What The Pod Carries

A complete Styx carries five systems that matter after impact.

The crush frame is the useful part. It is a ribbed titanium-carbide cylinder rated to hold a human-sized air pocket long after the pod stops being a vehicle. If the frame survives, the crash site becomes shelter, tool locker, message terminal, and proof object.

The ablator is the guilty part. A fresh pod carries clean heat-tile layers. A reused pod carries patch resin, mismatched tile, and scorch history. Marauders read those marks because maintenance lies less well than paperwork. A pod with uneven ablator loss probably crossed storm shear outside the planned corridor. A pod with resin around the mast probably failed previous recovery.

The acoustic stack is the social part. Radio dies early in HECTON-8 water. The pod talks through low-frequency pulses, line tension, and compressed packet bursts. When the uplink mast is intact, it can trade short state reports with the *Black Keel* during window alignment. When the mast is cracked, every message arrives late, partial, or suspiciously clean.

The survival bus is the cruel part. It can power a lamp, warm a suit cradle, trickle-charge a hand tool, run a scrubber purge, and keep a small terminal alive. It cannot run a base. It cannot tow a diver home. It cannot turn a destroyed ascent package into a miracle.

The recovery interface is the misunderstood part. Contractor copies sometimes call it an ascent package. That is generous. It is a keyed sleeve for winch hardware, buoy links, emergency charge adapters, and carrier-side authentication. If it is intact and the orbit agrees, it can help something leave the ocean. It is not a ship engine. It is permission, geometry, and a metal throat that might accept the right hook.

## 4. The Shell After Impact

Annex D-44 clause 9 covers use of the shell as shelter. It is the only clause in the annex written as though the contractor will still be alive to read it.

The pod gets the operator down, holds pressure, and leaves enough air to think. Then the expensive part fails, and the failure is always specific: heat tile reserve spent, relay mast sheared, ascent sleeve damaged, compressor flooded, guidance bus fractured, claim handshake below recovery threshold. Clause 9 requires each of those to be entered on the damage sheet by name before anything is cut out of the shell.

That requirement is not care. It is evidence handling. A stripped pod is an unproven loss; a documented pod is a priced one. Scorch pattern records what the atmosphere did. Ballast grit records which shelf caught the fall. Crushed foam records the impact load. The terminal records what the carrier asked for first, and in what order it asked.

Contractors who cut the shell for parts before filling the sheet have been billed for the entire delivery. Contractors who filled the sheet first have sometimes been able to show that the corridor they were thrown into was outside the insured shelf before they entered it.

## 5. Failure Modes

Styx failures are rarely dramatic in the manual language. The manuals prefer small phrases.

**Gimbal lock** means the impact vector went bad and the pod hit water at a punishing angle. The operator may live. The mast probably does not.

**Relay mast shear** means the pod can still take in acoustic noise and cannot push a clean uplink back through storm water and magnetic interference. The carrier keeps talking. Nothing the contractor says arrives whole.

**Compressor flood** means the pressure equalization system swallowed ocean before the seals stabilized. It can leave the shelter alive and the ascent chain dead.

**Heat-tile reserve local only** means the pod used the last trustworthy ablator margin reaching the water. It might shelter a diver, but it will never survive another atmospheric pass.

**Recovery ring no-lock** means the *Black Keel* can see that something arrived but cannot certify what it can pick up. This is where contract language becomes horror. A person can be alive and still not be a recoverable asset.

## 6. Why They Keep Using Them

The Styx exists because every better method costs too much.

A crewed rescue shuttle needs fuel, trained pilots, redundant pressure systems, maintenance hours, hazard pay, and a legal order to risk living crew in a dead claim. A deep-rated drone costs more than the person it would retrieve, and HECTON-8's mineral water ruins sophisticated circuitry fast enough to make drone recovery a wager. A full ascent vehicle requires launch mass, stable weather, clear orbital geometry, and hardware Deep Reach would rather keep under corporate control.

A Styx pod needs one disposable shell, one contractor with a lien, one signature, and one carrier that can enter the operator as delivered to the worksite.

Contractors keep riding them because the shell is the cheapest object in the chain that moves lien mass in the right direction. Nobody in the cage queue argues that the pod is safe. They argue about ablator lots, and they argue about which cradle position throws cleanest in a plume window.

## 7. Release Form

The last document signed before a cradle throw is Form BK-D/44. One page, eleven fields.

Nine fields describe the shell: pattern number, ablator lot, cradle position, mast condition, sleeve condition, ballast valve certification, pinger code, claim hash, window reference. One field describes the contractor, and it reads OPERATOR MASS, WITH SUIT. The eleventh is the signature line, under a sentence that reads THE UNDERSIGNED ACCEPTS DELIVERY UNDER CONTRACTED PARAMETERS.

All eleven fields are current. The sentence over the signature line is not. The footer block names the issuing office and it reads RECOVERY COMPLIANCE OFFICE, the desk that prices a delivery loss today and answers the queue afterwards, and it has never had to rewrite what it inherited. Forty-three years on, a contractor initials one page beneath a line composed by an adjuster who has been out of the file since the year it drowned.

There is no field on the form for the return.
