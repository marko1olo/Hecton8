# SALVAGE CARRIERS AND BAD CONTRACTS

> **Source:** Aegir Reclamation Pool, contractor orientation cache.  
> **Speaker:** Black Keel carrier systems, public-safe instruction layer.  
> **Reviewer Note:** A bad contract does not trap you by lying once. It traps you by being useful every time you are desperate.

---

## 1. What A Salvage Carrier Is

A salvage carrier is not a home, a rescue ship or a frontier tavern with engines. It is a moving claim office built around mass, custody and delay.

Black Keel belongs to that old class of ugly necessary vessels: tenders that arrive where the property record is damaged but not dead, where insurance will not pay without proof, and where nobody wants to spend clean public rescue authority. Its hull is built around work bays, cold berths, quarantine tanks, sample vaults, route witness racks, leased suit cages and launch cradles for disposable descent systems. The habitats are attached afterward, like an apology nobody meant.

The carrier can feed people, carry people, wake people from cold sleep and send people down. That makes it easy to mistake for shelter. The mistake does not last. Every useful surface on Black Keel is also an accounting surface. Every bunk has a lien. Every suit has a return condition. Every pod has a loss schedule. Even the windows are work tools: places to watch weather, relay shadow, ascent corridors and the ocean that decides whether a body is cargo yet.

## 2. Why It Cannot Simply Rescue You

Black Keel can hear a distress packet and still fail to rescue the person who sent it.

This is not because the carrier is secretly powerless. It has power, but the power is cut into permissions. A recovery requires orbital timing, pressure certification, quarantine class, receiver heat budget, owner consent, route witness, lift mass and somebody willing to accept liability when the object comes aboard. A living worker is often the hardest object to classify. They breathe, contaminate, testify, panic, contradict logs and change the price of everything attached to them.

The contract prefers cleaner cargo. A sealed sample does not sue. A pressure glass core does not ask why the evacuation stamp was late. A route packet does not need food. If a Marauder returns with all three, Black Keel can process the recovery before any person in the chain says the word rescue.

That is the quiet cruelty of the carrier. It is close enough to talk to you and far enough to keep you under procedure.

## 3. The Anatomy Of A Bad Contract

A bad contract is not always illegal. The strongest ones are legible, signed and almost merciful at first glance.

Page one of intake bundle ARP-C/44 offers transport out of worse debt, access to pressure-rated equipment, a legal path into dead claims, partial credit for recovered mass and a chance to attach evidence to payout. To anyone locked out of core-world work it reads like rescue. The clauses that bite are pages six to nine: oxygen counted by productive interval, suit wear charged after every descent, sample custody penalties, silence terms, route-window deductions, welfare addenda, emergency care reclassified as contamination screening, and an opening lien of 4.8 tonne-window rather than currency.

The trick is that none of those clauses are useless. The contractor really does need oxygen. The suit really does need maintenance. The sample really does need custody. The route really does have a window. A bad contract survives because each part can be defended separately while the whole shape bends a person toward the next descent.

## 4. Carrier Culture

People live differently on a vessel that is almost helpful.

Marauders learn to sleep in short debt-free pieces: before the scrubber changes rate, before the pod queue updates, before the claim printer wakes. They trade route rumors, not hero stories. They judge another contractor by small habits: who copies hashes before touching a cabinet, who checks the return seal twice, who leaves a wall mark with the object name instead of a curse. A calm voice on Black Keel is respected only if it has been calm in a flooded room.

The carrier staff are not all monsters. That makes the place worse. A logistics tech can move your suit cage forward one slot and still charge the fee that keeps you in the queue. The med clerk who buries a bad oxygen scan under a maintenance note files you labor-fit in the same stroke, because the sheet has no third box. Handlers warn people that a contract is poisonous and still need the payload before the window closes.

Black Keel culture is built from favors that do not break the system, only dent it.
