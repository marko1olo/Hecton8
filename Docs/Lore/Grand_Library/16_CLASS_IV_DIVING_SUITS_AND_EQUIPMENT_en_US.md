# CLASS-IV PRESSURE SUITS AND ABYSSAL EQUIPMENT

> **Source:** Black Keel suit-cage registry, Deep Reach industrial diving certification fragments, contractor maintenance tags.  
> **Speaker:** Quartermaster service voice with unsafe procurement language removed.  
> **Reviewer note:** This is the technical anatomy of a Class-IV work system. For the lived culture of wearing one, see the abyssal-work article.

Class-IV is not a brand name. It is a pressure-work certification band.

That distinction saves lives. A Marauder who treats the suit as a model will look for the wrong promises: armor rating, weapon slot, advertised depth, upgrade tier. A handler who treats it as a certification band asks better questions. Which seals were changed? Which pressure cycles are still counted as valid? Which joint exceeded assist tolerance? Which scrubber wafer was paid for and which one was re-stamped? Which viewport edge has salt under the laminate?

The suit is not one machine. It is a stack of arguments that agree, briefly, to keep a human separate from the ocean.

## The Pressure Envelope

The part outsiders notice first is the hard frame: ribbed torso shell, shoulder yoke, hip ring, joint cages, ballast boots, spine mount and viewport crown. Those pieces give the suit its slow silhouette, but the pressure envelope is larger and stranger than the visible armor.

The true envelope includes soft bladders, gasket lips, glove bearings, condensate paths, helmet feed, scrubber seals, tool-port shutters and the little compression surfaces nobody praises until they fail. A Class-IV can look intact and already be losing the argument. Paint survives longer than trust. A polished plate may hide a creeping seal. A dented guard may be harmless if the gasket behind it never moved.

Deep Reach manuals call the envelope "redundant." Marauders prefer the word "negotiated." Redundancy implies a spare system waiting politely. Negotiation is closer to the truth: one seal accepts more load because another has aged, one hinge sticks because salt has chosen a side, one bladder patch holds because the worker did not twist too fast in the previous route.

The ocean does not break a suit all at once when it can teach every part to lie first.

## Breath, Heat And Waste

Black Keel does not issue air. It issues a temporary agreement among bottles, mix valves, carbon scrubbers, humidity traps, thermal mesh, firmware thresholds and the lungs of someone under debt.

The breathing stack is deliberately unromantic. Supply mix enters through certified bottles, pod transfer, receiver fill or emergency cartridge, depending on route and contract. Exhaled carbon dioxide is pulled through replaceable wafers that begin as chemistry and become a bill. Moisture condenses where it should, unless the suit is cold in the wrong place, dirty in the wrong groove, or tilted long enough for water to find a patient path.

Heat is not comfort. Heat is cognition. Cold fingers misread tools. Cold ribs breathe badly. Cold panic spends oxygen. The thermal mesh therefore competes with lamps, servos, receivers and sample refrigeration. A good suit does not keep a diver warm. It keeps judgment barely warm enough to remain useful.

Waste is the subject contractors joke about until the first long delay. The suit has urine handling, sweat channels, absorbent layers, odor capture and privacy systems that mostly work when the route is normal. When recovery slips, those systems become morale, infection control and one more way for the ledger to bill a body for staying alive.

## Movement, Ballast And Bad Grace

A Class-IV does not swim. It stands, braces, shuffles, climbs, drops and argues with the floor.

The boots carry ballast in stages because sudden lightness can kill as cleanly as sudden weight. A release that saves a worker from sinking into silt can slam them into wreckage overhead. A ballast lock that refuses to open can make a shallow step into a burial. This is why experienced divers listen to their feet. A boot that lands wrong is often the first honest sensor.

Servo assistance is conservative by design. The suit could be stronger in ways that would injure its occupant. A hand actuator that never yields can crush a glove against a hatch. A knee that overcorrects can turn a slip into a torn ligament. A shoulder that helps too eagerly can wrench the worker while the tool stays fixed. Class-IV movement is ugly because ugly is safer than graceful overconfidence.

For short vertical work, some suits carry burst jets or lift bottles. They are not flight systems. They are mistakes with handles: cross a gap, climb a collapsed lip, clear a brine shelf, escape a floor that stopped being floor. Every use changes heat, bubbles, sound, silt and debt.

## Seeing Without Believing The Glass

The viewport is small for reasons older than the Marauder trade. More glass means more vision, more stress, more cleaning, more failure surface and more faith in the wrong sense.

A Class-IV worker sees through layers: direct glass, suit sonar, pressure maps, tool vibration, receiver pings, tether tension, silt behavior, lamp backscatter and the small mechanical honesty of how a handle resists the hand. No single layer is trusted. The glass confirms. It does not rule.

The suit's warning system follows the same principle. The loud alarms are for people already late. Better operators notice quieter changes: a fan note that goes thin, a glove humidity trace that climbs after a turn, a pressure delta that refuses to settle, a heater draw that looks too clean, a sonar return with an empty edge. Machine honesty is rarely dramatic. Drama usually means the ocean has stopped negotiating.

Modern Marauder HUDs therefore avoid fantasy clarity. They are cramped, layered and sometimes ugly because truth underwater is partial. A clean interface that removes ambiguity may also remove the clue that keeps someone alive.

## Tools As Part Of The Suit

Class-IV equipment is not carried like camping gear. It is mounted, locked, routed, powered, balanced and logged as part of the suit's working body.

The common ports are not glamorous: cutter mount, drill mount, sample sleeve, tether hook, clamp rail, lamp socket, receiver socket, bolt splitter, seal pick, waste purge, external bottle latch. Each one has a custody implication. A sample taken through the wrong sleeve may still be chemically valid and legally dirty. A cutter used before its mount shock is logged may turn a clean salvage cut into a disputed one. A rescue hook that saves a worker can also prove which direction the worker was moving when the route failed.

Marauders call tools by verbs because nouns are how quartermasters price them. Cut. Hold. Mark. Vent. Scrape. Brace. Lift. Bag. Burn. Prove. The best equipment is boring until the exact second it becomes the difference between a route and a claim file.

Weapons exist, but the suit does not understand the moral difference. It sees draw, recoil, heat, mount stress, custody status and operator biometrics. If a tool becomes a weapon, the system records consequences before it records reasons.

## Failure Modes That Matter

Most Class-IV failures begin smaller than fear wants.

Seal creep means a gasket is still present but no longer honest. It may pass a pre-check and fail after kneeling, twisting or crossing a pressure band. Joint overassist means the suit is trying to be helpful faster than the body can tolerate. Scrubber channeling means carbon dioxide finds a cheaper path than chemistry expected. Heater shadow means one part of the body is warm enough to lie while another is already losing dexterity.

Viewport milk is not cosmetic. It can mean laminate stress, salt fogging, microfracture or a previous bad cleaning. Receiver wet-mouth means the acoustic socket has taken brine and may begin inventing confidence. Tool-port grit means a shutter did close, but not cleanly. Dead-sensor silence is worse than an alarm because it can feel like permission.

The worst failures are mixed. A cold hand misses a seal pick. The bad seal changes humidity. The humidity makes a warning noisy. The operator mutes the warning to hear a ping. The ping was late. The floor was not floor. The report later calls the loss environmental.

## Upgrades Are Tradeoffs

The public store page would call upgrades progression. Marauders call them new ways to owe money.

Better seals open routes and make every later inspection stricter. Stronger servos move heavier salvage and can punish bad posture. Cleaner acoustic receivers reveal more, including orders the carrier would prefer to phrase softly. Bigger batteries buy heat, light or tool time, but not all three. Improved sample sleeves protect evidence while making the carrier more interested in what you carried. Better ballast release saves lives and can also strand a worker against a ceiling, under a ledge or outside a recovery geometry.

Nothing upgrades the diver out of consequence. The point of Class-IV gear is not to become invulnerable. It is to move the line where attention fails.

That is why old handlers inspect upgraded suits with more suspicion, not less. A cheap suit fails honestly. A heavily modified one may fail in a language only its last owner understood.

## What The Player Takes Away

The Class-IV is the game's most intimate piece of worldbuilding because the player lives inside it.

Every hiss, warning, slow step, fogged edge, tool mount and billable consumable should teach the same lesson: survival is engineered, rented and recorded. The suit is a pressure boundary, a workplace, a witness and a debt instrument. It protects the player from the ocean while exposing them to every system that decided protection could be metered.

A good Class-IV does not make HECTON-8 less hostile. It makes hostility legible long enough for the player to choose what kind of evidence, salvage and body comes back.
