# CLASS-IV PRESSURE SUITS AND ABYSSAL EQUIPMENT

> **Source:** Black Keel suit-cage registry, Deep Reach industrial diving certification fragments, contractor maintenance tags.  
> **Speaker:** Quartermaster service voice with unsafe procurement language removed.  
> **Reviewer note:** This is the technical anatomy of a Class-IV work system. For the lived culture of wearing one, see the abyssal-work article.

Class-IV is a pressure-work certification band. The cage registry has no field for a model name and no field for advertised depth.

What it has is one card per suit. Cage 12 holds SUIT-C4-0417. Its current card reads: SEAL LOT 88-J PARTIAL / CYCLES VALID 61 OF 90 / LEFT SHOULDER ASSIST 1.14 AGAINST 1.10 TOLERANCE / SCRUBBER WAFER LOT RE-STAMPED / VIEWPORT EDGE SALT PRESENT / HOLD CLASS NONE. Five of those lines describe a defect. The sixth decides whether the suit leaves the cage. Handlers read the card from the bottom up for that reason, and the honest ones read it out loud to the contractor who has to wear it.

## The Pressure Envelope

The part outsiders notice first is the hard frame: ribbed torso shell, shoulder yoke, hip ring, joint cages, ballast boots, spine mount and viewport crown. Those pieces give the suit its slow silhouette, but the pressure envelope is larger and stranger than the visible armor.

The true envelope includes soft bladders, gasket lips, glove bearings, condensate paths, helmet feed, scrubber seals, tool-port shutters and the little compression surfaces nobody praises until they fail. A Class-IV can look intact and already be losing the argument. Paint survives longer than trust. A polished plate may hide a creeping seal. A dented guard may be harmless if the gasket behind it never moved.

Deep Reach certification fragment DR-DIV-4/ENV lists the envelope as "redundant." Marauders write "negotiated" over that word on the cage board, because redundancy implies a spare system waiting politely. One seal accepts more load because another has aged. One hinge sticks because salt has chosen a side. A hip bladder patch holds only as long as the wearer does not twist hard, and the patch on 0417 was applied in a work bay with a torn glove ring on the bench beside it.

Seal lot 88-J came back off a suit with a split hip bladder. The lot was never withdrawn. It was re-listed as inspected, and the inspection stamp is the same one used for new stock.

## Breath, Heat And Waste

Black Keel does not issue air. It issues a temporary agreement among bottles, mix valves, carbon scrubbers, humidity traps, thermal mesh, firmware thresholds and the lungs of someone under debt.

The breathing stack is deliberately unromantic. Supply mix enters through certified bottles, pod transfer, receiver fill or emergency cartridge, depending on route and contract. Exhaled carbon dioxide is pulled through replaceable wafers that begin as chemistry and become a bill. Moisture condenses where it should, unless the suit is cold in the wrong place, dirty in the wrong groove, or tilted long enough for water to find a patient path.

The thermal mesh competes with lamps, servos, receivers and sample refrigeration off one bus, and the cage board fills the split in before a suit leaves: MESH 38 PERCENT / LAMP 22 / RECEIVER 12 / SAMPLE HOLD 28. Nobody signs that row. It arrives pre-filled, and sample hold is never the number that gets cut. Cold fingers misread a seal pick; a cold rib cage breathes shallow and burns wafer faster than the work that earned it. Contractors who come back with usable hands usually admit they stole mesh percentage from the lamp and worked inside a smaller cone.

Waste is the subject contractors joke about until the first long delay. The suit has urine handling, sweat channels, absorbent layers, odor capture and privacy systems that mostly work when the route is normal. When recovery slips, those systems become morale, infection control and one more way for the ledger to bill a body for staying alive.

## Movement, Ballast And Bad Grace

A Class-IV does not swim. It stands, braces, shuffles, climbs, drops and argues with the floor.

The boots carry ballast in stages because sudden lightness can kill as cleanly as sudden weight. A release that saves a worker from sinking into silt can slam them into wreckage overhead. A ballast lock that refuses to open can make a shallow step into a burial. This is why experienced divers listen to their feet. A boot that lands wrong is often the first honest sensor.

Servo assistance is capped low because the suit could be stronger in ways that would injure its occupant. A hand actuator that never yields will crush a glove against a hatch lip, and the tolerance sheet lists that failure under equipment damage, not injury. Knees are worse: overcorrect on a slip and the ligament tears inside a joint cage nobody can open in the water. Assist tolerance on 0417's left shoulder is already over by 0.04, which is why the wear log has two entries for tool-side wrench in the same shift and no entry for the worker.

For short vertical work some suits carry burst jets or lift bottles, rated for one gap, one collapsed lip, one brine shelf, one floor that stopped being floor. The registry counts every firing: heat, bubble volume, acoustic signature, silt disturbance, and a line-item charge that posts before the contractor is back in the cage.

## Seeing Without Believing The Glass

The viewport is small for reasons older than the Marauder trade. More glass means more vision, more stress, more cleaning, more failure surface and more faith in the wrong sense.

A Class-IV worker sees through layers: direct glass, suit sonar, pressure maps, tool vibration, receiver pings, tether tension, silt behavior, lamp backscatter and the small mechanical honesty of how a handle resists the hand. No single layer is trusted. The glass confirms. It does not rule.

The alarm tiers are set for people who are already late. Earlier signs are quieter and unlisted: a fan note that goes thin, a glove humidity trace that climbs after a turn, a pressure delta that will not settle, a heater draw that reads too clean for the water temperature, a sonar return with an empty edge. Deep Reach certification never required any of those to be displayed, so contractors write them on the inside of the forearm cuff in grease pencil and hope the cuff comes back.

Marauder-modified readouts stay cramped and layered on purpose. A display that resolves ambiguity has to choose which reading to drop, and the dropped reading is usually the humidity trace, because it is the one that costs money to act on.

## Tools As Part Of The Suit

Class-IV equipment is not carried like camping gear. It is mounted, locked, routed, powered, balanced and logged as part of the suit's working body.

The common ports are not glamorous: cutter mount, drill mount, sample sleeve, tether hook, clamp rail, lamp socket, receiver socket, bolt splitter, seal pick, waste purge, external bottle latch. Each one has a custody implication. A sample taken through the wrong sleeve may still be chemically valid and legally dirty. A cutter used before its mount shock is logged may turn a clean salvage cut into a disputed one. A rescue hook that saves a worker can also prove which direction the worker was moving when the route failed.

Marauders call tools by verbs because nouns are how quartermasters price them. Cut. Hold. Mark. Vent. Scrape. Brace. Lift. Bag. Burn. Prove. The issue sheet uses nouns and part numbers, and the two vocabularies meet only in the wear log, where a cutter contact billed as consumable stock is the same object that proved which side of a hatch was cut first.

Weapons exist, but the suit does not understand the moral difference. It sees draw, recoil, heat, mount stress, custody status and operator biometrics. If a tool becomes a weapon, the system records consequences before it records reasons.

## Failure Modes That Matter

Most Class-IV failures begin smaller than fear wants.

Seal creep means a gasket is still present but no longer honest. It may pass a pre-check and fail after kneeling, twisting or crossing a pressure band. Joint overassist means the suit is trying to be helpful faster than the body can tolerate. Scrubber channeling means carbon dioxide finds a cheaper path than chemistry expected. Heater shadow means one part of the body is warm enough to lie while another is already losing dexterity.

Viewport milk is not cosmetic. It can mean laminate stress, salt fogging, microfracture or a previous bad cleaning. Receiver wet-mouth means the acoustic socket has taken brine and may begin inventing confidence. Tool-port grit means a shutter did close, but not cleanly. Dead-sensor silence is worse than an alarm because it can feel like permission.

The worst failures are mixed. A cold hand misses a seal pick. The bad seal changes humidity. The humidity makes a warning noisy. The operator mutes the warning to hear a ping. The ping was late. The floor was not floor. The report later calls the loss environmental.

## Upgrades Are Tradeoffs

The pool equipment catalog files upgrades under progression. The cage board files them under service grade, and every grade change adds a line to the return condition.

Better seals open routes and make every later inspection stricter. Stronger servos move heavier salvage and punish bad posture in the same shift. Cleaner acoustic receivers pass more traffic, including carrier instructions the contractor was never meant to hear in full sentences. A bigger cell buys heat, light or tool time; the split above is what it buys them out of. Improved sample sleeves protect custody and make the receiving desk far more interested in what came up inside them.

Ballast release is the honest example. Grade 3 release saves a worker sinking into silt and can slam the same worker into wreckage overhead, or leave them outside the recovery geometry printed on their own descent card. Both outcomes are inside certification. Only one of them has a form.

Old handlers inspect a modified suit longer than a cheap one. A stock suit fails against a published tolerance sheet. A modified suit fails against whatever its last owner decided the tolerance was, and that number is rarely written anywhere in the cage.

## Return Condition

Every suit is priced against the card it left with.

The return desk fills six fields: seal set condition, cycles consumed, assist tolerance exceedances, wafer lot spent, viewport laminate state, and equipment mass unaccounted. A suit that comes back without its occupant is completed the same way, and the last field carries the difference. SUIT-C4-0388 arrived on a winch line in the second window of this rotation. Its return card reads ENVELOPE INTACT / WAFER LOT SPENT / MASS UNACCOUNTED 71 KG. Nothing on the form records what the 71 kg was, because the form has no field for people.

Handlers keep a second book that nobody signs. It lists which cage a suit came out of, who wore it before, what the wearer wrote on the forearm cuff, and which defect the official card carried as issuable. They keep it because in eleven rotations the card has not once explained a failure early enough to act on.
