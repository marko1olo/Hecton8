## Source Brief

Aegir descent-loss priority is a local claim-law class for remote descent failures. It exists because Aegir routes are slow, weathered, expensive, and often owned by companies that would rather certify what can be recovered than admit what cannot be saved.

The clause does not say "abandon workers." It says something colder: if a descent vehicle is non-returning, carrier burn windows are unsafe or unavailable, local life-support response is uncertified, and biological recovery cannot be guaranteed, then legally certified priority moves to evidence preservation, equipment inventory, beacon continuity, corpse-chain marking when possible, and claim reconciliation.

It is how a living problem becomes a recoverable file.

## Public / Website Article

Remote systems need ugly law. That is the sentence every claim office uses before it explains why an emergency became inventory.

Aegir descent-loss priority was written for routes where a failed descent cannot be answered by immediate mass. The Aegir system has carrier windows, storm-driven optical limits, deep gravity costs, local claim boundaries, and the ordinary cruelty of distance. A ship may be able to hear a disaster and still be unable, unwilling, or legally uncertified to burn for it.

The priority class activates when a descent craft, work pod, cargo cage, or personnel drop chain stops returning clean status from a registered surface or subsurface site. A local registry then asks a sequence of questions:

- Is biological recovery certified by this route?
- Is carrier burn allocation available inside the live window?
- Is local life-support response reachable and bonded?
- Are beacons, logs, bodies, equipment, and claim marks still recoverable?
- Which evidence objects must be preserved before the site becomes legally ambiguous?

If the answers fail in the expected direction, the site is moved out of rescue language and into descent-loss priority. The wording is careful. It does not call people dead. It does not promise they are alive. It says the route can no longer certify extraction. It orders the parts of the disaster that can still be counted to remain countable.

Deep Reach did not invent the class, but it knew how to live inside it. A descent-loss stamp lets a company acknowledge that something happened without accepting full biological recovery liability. It lets equipment become a claim object, logs become route evidence, and names become queue entries. In the right hearing, an intact Luyten hash can make that maneuver expensive. In the water, the stamp is colder than silence because it proves the system had a word ready.

For a Marauder, the practical rule is simple: do not throw away stamped debris. A bent placard, a cargo bracket receipt, or a half-drowned terminal notice can prove when the priority was invoked and who benefited from the downgrade.

## Player Codex Entry

This placard carries an Aegir descent-loss priority stamp. The clause appears when a registered descent asset stops returning valid status and the route refuses to certify biological recovery. Certified action moves to evidence, inventory, beacon, and claim preservation.

The language is written to avoid naming abandonment. It marks the site as unresolved, then tells every paid system what can still be counted. Deep Reach procedure can use that gap to contain a disaster while denying rescue liability.

If this stamp predates the final local failure, it may prove that recovery priority was downgraded while data, equipment, or personnel were still present.

## Spoiler / Writer Truth

Descent-loss priority turns human absence into process. It is not a secret murder order and not a rescue system. It is a lawful pressure valve that lets Aegir operators, claim holders, and Deep Reach route procedure move from living-worker urgency to recoverable-value accounting when rescue is expensive, uncertified, or politically inconvenient.

Use this class when the player needs to understand why the colony can be recognized as a disaster while still being treated as a claim site. The best use is emotional contradiction: the terminal sounds calm, but the player is standing in water where the clause was applied to human rooms.
